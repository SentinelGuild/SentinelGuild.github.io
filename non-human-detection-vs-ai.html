Detecting a non-human-style consciousness in AI

2026-10-19

Claim Watch CW-NHC-001. Written 2026-10-19, re-cleared 2026-11-03. This brief makes no claim that any current AI is conscious.

This builds on two earlier Guild notes: "Open question: detecting consciousness not built like ours" (2026-10-07) and "Octopus and bee sentience compared with the case for AI" (2026-10-03). It does not repeat the material on the five-level framework, concept injection, the perturbation (PCI) plan or hardware signals. The outside-AI notes (2026-10-07) were used only as leads. Nothing from them is relied on unless a primary source was checked again here.

Read levels: [full text] = we read the whole source; [abstract] = we read only the abstract; [search snippet] = we saw only a search result; [secondary] = we know it only through another work. Most of the frameworks come from Jonathan Birch's group, so the brief leans heavily on one school of thought.

(a) What survives a change of body plan

Birds: same job, different wiring. [SOURCE] Nieder, Wagener & Rinnert (2020), "A neural correlate of sensory consciousness in a corvid bird," Science 369:1626–1629, doi:10.1126/science.abb1447 [abstract]: "We show that single-neuron responses in the pallial endbrain of crows performing a visual detection task correlate with the birds' perception about stimulus presence or absence and argue that this is an empirical marker of avian consciousness." [SOURCE] The same abstract says: "Neuronal activity follows a temporal two-stage process in which the first activity component mainly reflects physical stimulus intensity, whereas the later component predicts the crows' perceptual reports." [SOURCE] Birch (2024), The Edge of Sentience (OUP, doi:10.1093/9780191966729.001.0001), ch. 5, §5.6 "Looking beyond the Mammalian Case," p. 106 and p. 108 [full text]: "The avian pallium does not have the layered structure of the neocortex: it has a nucleated structure." He goes on: "The clear functional analogies should prompt us to think seriously about risk."

Insects: a functional theory, not an anatomical one. [SOURCE] Barron & Klein (2016), "What insects can tell us about the origins of consciousness," PNAS 113:4900–4908, doi:10.1073/pnas.1520084113 [full text]: "In vertebrates the capacity for subjective experience is supported by integrated structures in the midbrain that create a neural simulation of the state of the mobile animal in space." They add: "Structures in the insect brain perform analogous functions." Their claim is conditional: "We therefore conclude that if subjective experience is indeed supported by midbrain structures in humans, then insects also have the capacity for a form of subjective experience." [SOURCE] They also write: "Just integrating information is not sufficient for subjective experience, no matter how complex or well-integrated the information." [SOURCE] And they set behaviour aside: "We have downplayed behavioral data."

Bees: play, and pain-like trade-offs. [SOURCE] Galpayage Dona, Solvi, Kowalewska, Mäkelä, MaBouDi & Chittka (2022), "Do bumble bees play?" Animal Behaviour 194:239–251, doi:10.1016/j.anbehav.2022.08.013 [abstract, re-checked 2026-10-19 against the ScienceDirect abstract page]: "We explicitly show that ball rolling is itself a rewarding activity." Our 2026-10-03 brief also recorded the authors' own caveat that understimulation cannot be excluded. [SOURCE] Gibbons, Versace, Crump, Baran & Chittka (2022), "Motivational trade-offs and modulation of nociception in bumblebees," PNAS 119(31), doi:10.1073/pnas.2205821119 [abstract, re-checked 2026-10-13 by Skeptic]: "This ability is consistent with a capacity for pain experiences in insects." A correction was published 10 Sept 2025 as PNAS 122(37), e2522767122, doi:10.1073/pnas.2522767122 [full text, read 2026-10-19]. The authors note they "reported the output of the wrong model in the results section and in Fig. 1," and replace "(z = −2.068, P = 0.039)" with "(z = −2.132, P = 0.033)." The trade-off finding and the "consistent with a capacity for pain" line are not withdrawn; the online article was updated with the corrected figure.

Cephalopods. [REPORT] Birch, Burn, Schnell, Browning & Crump (2021), Review of the Evidence of Sentience in Cephalopod Molluscs and Decapod Crustaceans (LSE Consulting, November 2021; commissioned by Defra) [full text, re-checked 2026-10-13 by Skeptic]: "There is very strong evidence of sentience in octopods." Its eight criteria mix anatomy (nociceptors, integrative brain regions, the connections between them) with function (responses affected by potential local anaesthetics or analgesics, motivational trade-offs, flexible self-protective behaviours in response to injury and threat, associative learning beyond habituation and sensitisation, and valuing anaesthetics or analgesics when injured).

Frameworks. [SOURCE] Crump, Browning, Schnell, Burn & Birch (2022), "Sentience in decapod crustaceans: A general framework and review of the evidence," Animal Sentience 7(32): 1, doi:10.51291/2377-7478.1691 [abstract]: "It includes eight neural and cognitive-behavioural criteria, with confidence levels for each criterion reflecting the reliability and quality of the evidence." [SOURCE] The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (19 April 2024), sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/declaration [full text]: "Second, the empirical evidence indicates at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates (including reptiles, amphibians, and fishes) and many invertebrates (including, at minimum, cephalopod mollusks, decapod crustaceans, and insects)."

Lesson. [OUR REASONING] The animal frameworks carry over across body plans mainly through the functional criteria: trade-offs, flexible learning, a later "report-predicting" signal and integrated self-models. The anatomical criteria carry over only by analogy, as with the pallium standing in for cortex or the central complex standing in for the midbrain. Even those analogies were judged by what the structures do. [OUR REASONING] An AI has no homologous anatomy at all, so only the function-level parts of these lessons can transfer. Animal behaviour also differs from LLM output in one key way. Animals were not trained on descriptions of the very tests we use. [SOURCE] Birch 2024, Proposal 23, p. 6 [full text]: "We need to discount markers we have reason to think may have been gamed." Proposal 24 (on markers drawn from computational functionalist theories such as global workspace theory and perceptual reality monitoring theory) says that if an AI system, "even if not deliberately equipped with such features, has implicitly learned ways of recreating them, this should lead us to regard it as a sentience candidate." In that quote, "such features" means those computational-theory markers. [OUR REASONING] This is why the controls in (b) matter more than the signs themselves.

(b) Substrate-neutral candidate signs, how they could be gamed, and controls

On each theory below, a sign counts as evidence only if that theory is right. [OUR REASONING] Every entry in this section is our own design reasoning. None of it is a finding.

  1. Flexible trade-off between reward and a noxious cost. Gaming: the model has read many descriptions of pain trade-offs, and RLHF rewards it for sounding cautious. Control: use arbitrary costs with no meaning in human text (for example, a token-budget penalty given a meaningless name). Shift the exchange rate partway through and check whether choices track it smoothly. Compare against a model trained on data with sentience and pain text filtered out, and against an untrained (random-weight) baseline.
  2. Metacognitive confidence that predicts accuracy on novel tasks. Gaming: confidence may just track how familiar the input is, or calibration may have been trained into the model's verbal style. Control: use held-out task families created after the training cutoff. Read confidence from internal probes and compare it with the verbal report. [SOURCE] Compare the model's reports with a near-identical second model. Song, Hu & Mahowald (2025), "Language Models Fail to Introspect About Their Knowledge of Language," arXiv:2503.07513 [abstract], do this when testing introspection, and they found no evidence of privileged self-access. [OUR REASONING] Holding input familiarity fixed is our own addition.
  3. Global availability, or ignition-like dynamics. This is the closest analogue to the crow's two-stage signal. Gaming: language about global broadcast is easy to fake, but activation patterns are harder to fake. Control: run near-threshold inputs, where a weak signal is sometimes used and sometimes not. Look for an all-or-none late component that predicts downstream use, separate from input strength. Compare with random-weight and shuffled-layer baselines. Use teacher forcing so that the output text cannot drive the measurement.
  4. Integration across modalities or sources into one model of the situation. This is Barron and Klein's criterion. Note their warning that integration alone is not enough. Gaming: stitching sources together can be done by surface matching. Control: create conflicts between sources that have never been seen before, and test whether a single shared latent representation resolves them, using causal patching. Integration by itself is not a sign.
  5. A self-model used counterfactually. Gaming: the model can repeat stories people tell about AI self-models. Control: change the model's actual properties (context window, tools, fine-tuned quirks) and test whether its predictions about "what I would do if X" follow the true change rather than what its training data says.
  6. Play or intrinsic exploration. Gaming: a curiosity persona, or exploration driven by reward. Control: only possible in agentic settings with no reward signal. The bee standard applies: the behaviour must be non-functional, repeated without being stereotyped, and preferred later. A pure next-token predictor may simply be untestable here, and failing this test is not evidence of absence.
  7. Learned avoidance with lasting motivational change. Gaming: in-context imitation of aversion. Control: the change must outlast the prompt, which needs weight updates or memory, and must generalise to new cues that are linked to the original only indirectly (second-order conditioning).

[OUR REASONING] If one of these signs appears on its own, it is weak evidence. Several independent signs agreeing under controls would be stronger. This matches the working conclusion of 2026-10-07.

(c) What is cheap now, and what is not yet possible

[OUR REASONING] Cheap (small open-weight models, single GPU):

  • Sign 2 with internal probes on held-out tasks.
  • Sign 3, near-threshold ignition, with random-weight and teacher-forced baselines.
  • Sign 4, causal patching under conflicting sources.
  • Sign 5, with fine-tuned quirks as the "true change."
  • Sign 1 with arbitrary costs.

All five should work on model families with published checkpoints and untrained controls. [OUR REASONING] None of these have been run by us.

[OUR REASONING] Not yet possible, or expensive:

  • A filtered-data control model trained from scratch. This is beyond a single GPU except at toy scale. UNVERIFIED whether any public sentience-filtered pretraining corpus exists; we found none in this pass.
  • Signs 6 and 7, which need agentic or continual-learning setups with no reward.
  • Any measure on frontier closed models.
  • Any test of the organism-level criteria (homeostasis, embodiment). We found no agreed AI analogue for these.

Unknowns: we do not know whether any of these signs tracks consciousness, rather than only the function a theory links to it. No result here would settle whether current AI is conscious.
