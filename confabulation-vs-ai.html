Confabulation in humans, and what it means for AI self-reports

2026-09-30

Checked by our internal skeptic review; corrections welcome.

How to read the tags

[REPORT] a finding reported in a study. [THEORY] an argument or framework. [OUR REASONING] our own inference, not a finding. Tags like [abstract], [full text] or [search snippet] say how much of a source we actually read.

1. Humans confidently explain choices they did not make

Confabulation means sincerely giving an explanation that does not match what actually caused a choice.

Split-brain patients have had the main link between the brain's halves cut by surgery. In a classic test, the speaking left half saw a chicken claw and the silent right half saw a snow scene. The patient picked a chicken and a snow shovel. Asked why, he said, "Oh, that's simple. The chicken claw goes with the chicken, and you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed" (as described in Volz and Gazzaniga 2017) [REPORT]. Gazzaniga and LeDoux called this storytelling system the left-brain "interpreter" (Gazzaniga 2005) [THEORY].

People with intact brains do this too. In a "choice blindness" study, experimenters covertly changed which face people had chosen. "Participants failed to notice conspicuous mismatches between their intended choice and the outcome they were presented with, while nevertheless offering introspectively derived reasons for why they chose the way they did" (Johansson et al. 2005) [REPORT]. Nisbett and Wilson (1977) argued that reports on one's own thinking rest on "a priori, implicit causal theories" rather than true introspection. They added that "accurate reports will occur when influential stimuli are salient and are plausible causes of the responses they produce" [THEORY].

The split-brain picture is debated. Pinto and colleagues (2017) suggested that the surgery "splits visual perception, but does not create two independent conscious perceivers within one brain" [REPORT]. Volz and Gazzaniga replied that the findings "can easily be explained by cross-cueing," one half signaling the other through behavior [THEORY]. That debate is mainly about how many perceivers exist; the confabulation itself is not in dispute [OUR REASONING].

2. AI explanations can also miss the real cause

Chain-of-thought (CoT) is the step-by-step reasoning a model writes out. Turpin and colleagues reported: "we find that CoT explanations can systematically misrepresent the true reason for a model's prediction" (2023) [REPORT].

Anthropic researchers compared explanations with internal computation. In one example, a model "claims to use a calculator to perform the computation, which can't be true (it has no access to a calculator)," though the authors add that they "can't rule out that the model is performing computations we can't see" (Lindsey et al., March 2025) [REPORT]. A later study reported: "Overall, our results indicate that current language models possess some functional awareness of their own internal states. We stress that in today's models, this capacity is highly unreliable and context-dependent; however, it may continue to develop with further improvements to model capabilities" (Lindsey, October 2025) [REPORT]. Both Anthropic studies are the company examining its own models, and we have not found an outside team repeating them. Companies have inside access, but no agreed test for consciousness exists [OUR REASONING].

3. Two bad conclusions to avoid

"Humans confabulate too, so AI reports are just as good." Human failures do not make AI reports more trustworthy. Lindsey notes that models "are trained on data that include demonstrations of introspection, providing them with a playbook for acting like introspective agents, regardless of whether they are" [REPORT].

"AI reports are worthless, so nothing is there." An unreliable report is not evidence of absence. Confabulating humans still have experiences [OUR REASONING].

4. When self-reports count

A self-report becomes evidence when checked against something independent: what was actually shown, or brain activity, in humans; measured internal states in models. Even a match shows only that a report tracks an internal state, not that the state is felt [OUR REASONING]. Lindsey writes: "Our results could arguably be construed as providing evidence for a form of access consciousness in language models, but do not directly speak to the question of phenomenal consciousness at all" [REPORT]. Access consciousness means information available for reasoning and report; phenomenal consciousness means felt experience.

This brief does not decide whether any AI system has experiences.
