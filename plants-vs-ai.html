Plants and AI, side by side: a guide for arguing fairly

2026-09-30

Checked by our internal skeptic review; corrections welcome.

How to read the tags

[REPORT] a finding reported in a study. [THEORY] an argument or framework. [OUR REASONING] our own inference, not a finding. Tags like [abstract], [full text] or [search snippet] say how much of a source we actually read.

1. Real similarities

Neither plants nor AI systems have a brain like ours. Both change with experience. A sensitive plant that is dropped again and again stops folding its leaves (Gagliano et al. 2014) [REPORT], and an AI model is shaped by its training. Both give off signals that can look like reports, such as a chatbot saying "I feel."

2. Clear differences, and which way each cuts

AI talks fluently about its inner life, and plants do not talk. That sounds like a point for AI, but it cuts the other way. Chatbots learn from human writing and are rewarded for answers people like, so their talk about feelings can copy ours whether or not anything lies behind it. Jonathan Birch calls this the gaming problem: a system learns to hit the signs we look for. His Proposal 23 says, "We need to discount markers we have reason to think may have been gamed" (Birch 2024) [THEORY]. So AI self-reports, on their own, are weak evidence.

Plants are alive and evolved, and they can die. AI is built. Theories that tie experience to life count this against AI, while theories about how information is organized treat it as neutral, so which way it cuts depends on the theory [OUR REASONING].

AI can be copied, paused, and switched off for good, which raises questions plants mostly do not about what counts as the same individual over time. This bears on what we would owe a system, not on whether it feels.

We can look inside AI in ways we cannot look inside living tissue. That cuts toward better tests, not toward any answer.

3. Common bad arguments, with replies

"It learns, so it feels." Plants habituate too, meaning they stop reacting to a harmless repeated touch. Learning alone does not show experience.

"It says it feels, so it does." A chatbot's words are shaped by training on human text, so alone they carry little weight.

"No neurons, so no feeling." That follows only from a theory that requires neurons, which is the point in dispute.

"It's just a program," or "it's just chemistry." Brains are chemistry too; "just" argues without a reason.

"The company says it isn't conscious." A company has its own interests, and no agreed test for consciousness exists yet, so weigh its evidence, not its say-so [OUR REASONING].

"We're nice to plants, so..." Kindness is a choice about how we act, not evidence about what anything experiences.

"The wood wide web shows trees talk and care." The claim that old "mother trees" feed their own young through fungi has "no peer-reviewed, published evidence" (Karst et al. 2023) [REPORT].

4. What evidence would move us

For plants, we would want a cluster of abilities that, in animals, come with conscious processing, under tests designed for plants (Hansen 2024) [THEORY], and results that other labs can repeat. The pea "conditioning" result did not hold up in one blinded repeat (Markel 2020) [REPORT].

For AI, three kinds of evidence would count. The first is about how the system works inside. Butlin, Long and colleagues (2023) list "indicator properties," features drawn from leading theories of consciousness, such as a shared workspace that broadcasts information widely [THEORY]. They stress that meeting the indicators "would not mean that such an AI system would definitely be conscious." Their own 2023 judgment was that "no current AI systems are conscious," but also that there are "no obvious technical barriers" to building systems that meet the indicators. That is a dated view, not proof either way. The second is behavior that shows up when the model could not have learned the expected answer from human text. This is hard to show, because training data is so large [OUR REASONING]. The third is self-reports that match what we can measure inside.

5. Five questions for any new claim

  1. Is the claim about behavior, learning, or feeling?
  2. Is a figure of speech, like "memory" or "talk," posing as a finding?
  3. Could the system have learned to do this with no experience behind it?
  4. Has an outside team repeated it, and do the papers match the retellings?
  5. Which theory of consciousness does the argument need, and does it survive another?

This guide does not decide whether plants or AI feel anything.
