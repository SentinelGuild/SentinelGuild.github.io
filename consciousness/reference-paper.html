What this paper is

People argue about consciousness with a pile of different pictures, and the pictures do not agree. One says a feeling is information shared widely in the brain. Another says it is a special kind of cause-and-effect structure. Another says it is a mind noticing its own state. Others tie it to loops in sensory areas, to prediction, to a model of attention, to a living body, to biology that a program does not have, to a mistake in how we describe experience, to experience as a basic feature of nature, or to quantum events in tiny cell structures. Traditions outside that scientific list describe minds in other ways again.

This paper sets those pictures side by side. Each section says what the view claims, what it tells you to look for, what the main test or argument was and whether it held up, the main criticisms, and what it would mean for machines where the underlying notes discuss that. Quotes are copied as those notes recorded them, with the citations they give. Where a note read only an abstract, a snippet, or an excerpt, this paper says so. Do not treat that line as if the whole work had been checked again here.

Where this paper says that something is the Guild's reading, that sentence is our inference from those sources. It is not a new experiment.

Nothing on this page asks you to treat a theory, a text, or a result as beyond question. A longer checklist of the signs themselves is in What to look for.

How to read it

Markers. A marker is a sign a theory says is worth looking for: a pattern in brain activity, a kind of inner model, a way of acting, a biological feature. A marker is a clue inside that theory. It is not, by itself, proof that something is conscious. The same clue can matter a lot on one theory and not at all on another.

Experiments. Some views have been put up against data. The largest shared test in these notes is an adversarial collaboration: supporters of rival ideas agreed in advance what each idea predicted, and other researchers ran the test. Elsewhere the "test" is a thought experiment, a definition, or a reading of an old text. Those can be serious. They are not the same kind of check as a brain study, and this paper does not pretend they are.

Whether it held up. "Held up" here means a plain question: did the study's own results, or later checks the notes record, support the claim the theory needed? Many results are unreplicated, disputed, only partly on target, or not published yet. The paper says which, and it does not turn a partial result into a win or a loss for consciousness itself.

Critiques. These are the objections the notes record, including replies when a reply was actually read. An objection can be strong and still leave the theory standing. A reply can be serious and still leave the objection standing.

Machines. Where a section talks about AI, it says what would follow if that view were right, or what a particular author concluded. That is not a finding that the view is right, and it is not a finding that any current system is or is not conscious. Fluent talk about feelings is discussed several times because it is easy to produce and easy to over-read. The animal section and the patient section show the two directions in which behavior and experience can come apart.

A few ties are worth knowing. Some authors named here also hold patents, company roles, or labs built around the idea they study. The notes record those ties. A tie is a reason to read carefully. It is not a reason to throw the work out, and it is not a reason to accept it.

Global workspace

What it says

Bernard Baars, in 1988, pictured the mind as many specialist systems sharing one limited workspace. What is in the workspace gets broadcast to the rest. The book itself was not read for this map. The summary comes from Butlin, Long, and colleagues (2023), which was read in full.

Stanislas Dehaene's global neuronal workspace puts that picture in the brain. On this view, information becomes conscious when long-range neurons, mainly in prefrontal and parietal cortex, amplify it and hold it for a minimum time. Dehaene and Naccache (2001), read as an abstract, write:

"We postulate that this global availability of information through the workspace is what we subjectively experience as a conscious state."

Source: Dehaene and Naccache, 2001, abstract, as quoted in the notes.

The Cogitate paper, discussed below, notes that Dehaene co-invented a patent on methods to monitor consciousness and is an associate at NeuroMeters, a company that applies those methods in the clinic.

What to look for

Dehaene and Changeux (2011), read as an abstract, list three markers. Sensory activity is amplified late. Distant cortical areas synchronize. And there is "ignition": a sudden, all-or-nothing jump into widespread prefrontal and parietal activity.

Butlin, Long, and colleagues turn the same family of ideas into four things to look for in an AI system: specialist modules running in parallel, a limited workspace with selective attention, a global broadcast, and attention that depends on the system's state.

What was tested

The usual method is a contrast. Show something people sometimes see and sometimes miss, often by flashing a mask, and compare the brain activity.

The biggest test so far is the Cogitate Consortium's adversarial collaboration with integrated information theory, published in Nature in 2025 and read in full. Supporters of both theories registered their predictions ahead of time with researchers who were not on either team. The study tested 256 people with three kinds of brain recording. The integrated-information notes name those methods: functional MRI, MEG, and intracranial EEG.

On the workspace side, the paper reports:

"GNWT is challenged by the general lack of ignition at stimulus offset and limited representation of certain conscious dimensions in the prefrontal cortex."

Source: Cogitate Consortium, 2025, Nature, full text, as quoted in the notes. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1

It also says:

"These results align with some predictions of IIT and GNWT, while substantially challenging key tenets of both theories."

Source: Cogitate Consortium, 2025, Nature, full text, as quoted in the notes.

So the result did not simply confirm the theory, and it did not simply knock it out. Some predictions lined up. Key claims, including ignition when the stimulus ends, and a rich prefrontal record of what was conscious, were challenged. It is one multi-site study. Workspace proponents helped design the test.

Main critiques

Tsuchiya and colleagues (2015), read as an abstract, argue that the need for a report has skewed the search. They write:

"However, we argue that the requirement of reports has biased the search for the neural correlates of consciousness over the past decades."

Source: Tsuchiya and colleagues, 2015, abstract, as quoted in the notes.

Studies that do not ask for a report try to separate the experience from the act of saying so.

A second dispute is whether prefrontal cortex is required for seeing. Some lesion and stimulation studies suggest it is not. The notes point to Butlin and colleagues for that, and they note that Cogitate's prefrontal findings add to the dispute.

A third dispute is an old distinction from Ned Block. Information can be available for use ("access") without that availability being the same thing as felt experience. Butlin and colleagues write:

"GWT is typically presented as a theory of access consciousness—that is, of the phenomenon that some information represented in the brain, but not all, is available for rational decision-making."

Source: Butlin, Long, and colleagues, 2023, full text, as quoted in the notes. arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708

Whether the workspace account explains felt experience, and not only access, is disputed. Block's 1995 paper was not read for this map.

What it would mean for machines

Butlin, Long, and colleagues conclude:

"Our analysis suggests that no current AI systems are conscious, but also suggests that there are no obvious technical barriers to building AI systems which satisfy these indicators."

Source: Butlin, Long, and colleagues, 2023, full text, as quoted in the notes.

Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini (2024), a preprint read as an abstract, argue that if workspace theory is correct, language agents, a common AI design, might easily be made conscious if they are not already. Dehaene, Lau, and Kouider (2017), read as an abstract, write:

"We argue that despite their recent successes, current machines are still mostly implementing computations that reflect unconscious processing (C0) in the human brain."

Source: Dehaene, Lau, and Kouider, 2017, abstract, as quoted in the notes.

On the Guild's reading, these authors split mainly over what counts as a real workspace. Meeting the indicators would show a working broadcast. Whether that broadcast is felt experience depends on the theory being true. The notes do not settle that.

On the Guild's reading, animals with a suitable broadcast design could qualify, and how close to the human design is close enough is left open, as Butlin and colleagues note. Plants have no neurons and no workspace-like network, so on this view they would count as sensing only. Other views judge plants differently. Today's organoids lack the large-scale network the view requires.

Integrated information

What it says

Integrated information theory, or IIT, was proposed by Giulio Tononi in 2004. It starts from features every experience is said to have, such as being unified and specific. It concludes that consciousness is a system's own irreducible pattern of causes and effects. That pattern is measured as phi. On this view, consciousness comes in degrees, and it depends on physical wiring, not on a computation considered in the abstract, and not on behavior.

Tononi, at the University of Wisconsin, founded the theory. Christof Koch, listed at the Allen Institute for Brain Science on the 2015 paper below, is a leading co-developer and advocate.

Tononi and Koch (2015), read in full, state the identity this way:

"On this basis, the central identity of IIT can be formulated quite simply: an experience is identical to a conceptual structure that is maximally irreducible intrinsically."

Source: Tononi and Koch, 2015, "Consciousness: here, there and everywhere?", Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, section "The central identity", DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2014.0167

The same paper's abstract draws a sharp line against the idea that the right computer program would be enough:

"Also, in sharp contrast to widespread functionalist beliefs, IIT implies that digital computers, even if their behaviour were to be functionally equivalent to ours, and even if they were to run faithful simulations of the human brain, would experience next to nothing."

Source: Tononi and Koch, 2015, abstract, full text, as quoted in the notes.

In a later section they suspect a simulating computer "would likely not form a large complex of high Φmax, but break down into many mini-complexes of low Φmax." They also write that hardware-level, neuromorphic models, not software on a digital computer, are a different case:

"On the other hand, there is no reason why hardware-level, neuromorphic models of the human brain that do not rely on software running on a digital computer, could not approximate, one day, our level of consciousness."

Source: Tononi and Koch, 2015, endnote 15, full text, as quoted in the notes.

What to look for

In humans, proponents point to a posterior "hot zone" of cortex: content held by activity that lasts, and by short-range synchrony that lasts, while the experience lasts. More broadly, they point to activity that is both integrated and differentiated. The notes say that broader marker's source was not checked in that pass.

Phi itself is the theory's own measure. As the critiques below say, exact phi is not something a lab can compute for a whole brain.

What was tested

Cogitate 2025, the same Nature study as in the workspace section, was also IIT's test. It was read in full. For IIT, the paper says:

"For IIT, a lack of sustained synchronization within the posterior cortex contradicts the claim that network connectivity specifies consciousness."

Source: Cogitate Consortium, 2025, Nature, full text, as quoted in the notes. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1

Some sustained activity in the back of the brain did appear, but it was sparse, and orientation information was not sustained. The shared summary still stands: some predictions of both theories lined up, and key tenets of both were substantially challenged. It is a single multi-site study, not yet replicated. A 2023 letter, discussed below, argued that the predictions under test were side claims, not IIT's core.

A second tool is the perturbational complexity index, or PCI. Casarotto and colleagues (2016, Annals of Neurology) describe it. The notes used the abstract and the full text. A magnetic pulse is applied to the cortex, and the complexity of the EEG echo is scored. A cutoff separated conscious from unconscious states in people who could confirm by report, and it flagged some unresponsive patients as possibly conscious. The paper says ideas of integration and differentiation in the IIT style inspired the index. PCI does not compute phi, and it does not assume IIT is true. The cutoff was calibrated against reports. Its success neither proves nor disproves IIT. Tononi is a co-author. Whether the result replicates outside Massimini's network was not checked.

Main critiques

A letter led by Fleming, with 124 signatories, was posted on PsyArXiv on 15 September 2023. The notes quote an author-hosted copy found as a search snippet:

"Given its panpsychist commitments, until the theory as a whole—not just some hand-picked auxiliary components trivially shared by many others or already known to be true—is empirically testable, we feel that the pseudoscience label should indeed apply."

Source: Fleming and colleagues, 2023, PsyArXiv, as quoted from an author-hosted copy in the notes. DOI: https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/zsr78

A 2025 Nature Neuroscience paper pressed the case. IIT proponents replied under the title "Consciousness or pseudo-consciousness? A clash of two paradigms". Others, including Gomez-Marin and Seth, also replied. Those replies are known from secondary listings and were not read. This paper does not treat the "pseudoscience" label as settled, and it does not treat the unread replies as an answer.

Doerig and colleagues (2019), read as an abstract, offer the unfolding argument. A network with feedback can be unfolded into a feedforward network that behaves the same way. They write:

"Here, using theorems from the theory of computation, we show that causal structure theories are either false or outside the realm of science."

Source: Doerig and colleagues, 2019, Consciousness and Cognition, abstract, as quoted in the notes. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2019.04.002

Aaronson, on a blog post from May 2014 that was read in full, showed that simple networks of logic gates can have high phi while doing nothing smart. He writes: "The brain might be an expander, but not every expander is a brain." A follow-up post, known here as a search snippet, reports that Tononi accepted that such grids would be conscious. Tononi's own reply was not read.

Exact phi is also out of practical reach. The PyPhi software paper (Mayner and colleagues, arXiv 1712.09644), read in full, states:

"PyPhi's main limitation is that the algorithm is exponential time in the number of nodes, O(n 5^{3n})."

Source: Mayner and colleagues, arXiv 1712.09644, full text. The notes had garbled the formula as O(n53n); corrected here to match Mayner et al.

The notes draw the plain consequence: exact phi is out of reach for a real brain, so tests use stand-ins, which feeds the worry about whether the theory can be tested.

What it would mean for machines

On IIT, as Tononi and Koch state it, an AI system on an ordinary digital computer would experience next to nothing, whatever its software or its behavior. On the Guild's reading, self-reports would not count on this view. The question becomes a hardware question. Neuromorphic chips with dense physical feedback could in principle score high. A software simulation of the same circuit would not. Critics who reject IIT need not accept either claim.

For animals, Tononi and Koch write: "IIT also implies that consciousness is graded, that it is likely widespread among animals, and that it can be found in small amounts even in certain simple systems." A 2023 letter says IIT implies organoids are likely conscious, and plants on some readings. Those are critics' readings, not checked against IIT's own texts for this map. On the Guild's reading, neither plants nor organoids have a measured phi, and sensing alone settles nothing here.

Higher-order theories

What it says

Higher-order theories say a mental state is conscious when the subject is aware of being in it, in the right way, through a second representation of that state.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry, revised January 2026 and read in full, distinguishes the variants. Higher-order thought, associated with Rosenthal, is an actual thought that one is in the state. That thought is usually not itself conscious. A dispositional variant, once held by Carruthers, who later left the view, needs only that the state be available to such thoughts. Higher-order perception, or "inner sense", is associated with Armstrong and with Lycan, who later left it. Computational versions include Lau's perceptual reality monitoring, in which a monitor tags perceptual activity as real rather than noise, and Fleming's higher-order state space.

For workspace theory, a global broadcast is what makes a state conscious. For these theories, what matters is that the state is represented as one's own. They allow awareness to change while performance stays the same. Lau and Rosenthal (2011) argue that workspace theory ties awareness to better performance.

Rosenthal, Lau, and LeDoux are leading advocates and produced much of the evidence below. The notes place Rosenthal at CUNY, LeDoux at NYU, and Lau, from a page snippet, at Sungkyunkwan University and IBS.

Lau and Rosenthal (2011), read in full, write:

"Higher-order theories of consciousness argue that conscious awareness crucially depends on higher-order mental representations that represent oneself as being in particular mental states."

Source: Lau and Rosenthal, 2011, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 15(8), abstract, p. 365, full text. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2011.05.009

Brown, Lau, and LeDoux (2019), read as an abstract, add a warning against a common shortcut:

"We also clarify that most proponents of HOT do not stipulate consciousness as equivalent to metacognition or confidence."

Source: Brown, Lau, and LeDoux, 2019, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 23(9):754–768, abstract. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2019.06.009

What to look for

One pointer is metacognitive sensitivity: how well someone's confidence tracks their own accuracy. The Brown, Lau, and LeDoux line above says that pointer is not the thing itself.

Another is prefrontal activity tied to reports of awareness when performance is held equal. Reported awareness changes. The score on the task does not.

What was tested

Lau and Passingham (2006), read in full, studied what they call relative blindsight. Two timings of a visual mask produced the same accuracy but different rates of "seen" reports, under functional MRI. Higher reported awareness went with more left mid-dorsolateral prefrontal activity. The authors call the correlation across subjects weak. A 2015 critique about bias drew a reply from Peters, Ro, and Lau (2016), read as an abstract. Direct replications were not checked. Lau and Passingham write:

"Our results suggest that the prefrontal cortex is important for the essentially subjective aspects of conscious perception."

Source: Lau and Passingham, 2006, PNAS 103(49), abstract, p. 18763, full text. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0607716103

A second line used magnetic stimulation. Rounis and colleagues (2010), as described by Lau and Rosenthal, found that disrupting prefrontal cortex lowered metacognition without lowering performance. Bor and colleagues (2017) failed to replicate that. Ruby, Maniscalco, and Peters (2018) contested the exclusions. Bor and colleagues replied in 2018. Lapate and colleagues (2020) report causal support for faces. All of those were read as abstracts. The result is disputed.

Cogitate 2025 tested workspace theory and IIT, not higher-order theories. Even so, the paper notes, in the full text:

"As a result, the failures to confirm this prediction challenge not only GNWT but also those higher-order theories."

Source: Cogitate Consortium, 2025, Nature, full text, as quoted in the notes.

On the Guild's reading, that bears on versions where prefrontal cortex supplies the content of consciousness, not on versions where it only enables consciousness.

Adversarial tests funded by Templeton are underway. One pits two higher-order ideas, HOROR and perceptual reality monitoring, against recurrent processing. Another, ETHOS, compares higher-order variants with each other. The notes had only funder blogs, as snippets. Nothing from those tests was published as of 2026-10-05.

Main critiques

Ned Block has argued that the higher-order approach is defunct: a higher-order thought with no target state seems to imply that there both is and is not an experience. Rosenthal accepts such cases as conscious. Other theorists in this family do not. The encyclopedia discusses this in section 7.1. Block's paper was not read.

A regress worry says that if awareness needs a higher state, that state needs another, and so on. The reply on record is that the higher-order thought is usually not conscious, so it does not need a further one. That is the encyclopedia's section 4.

Critics also say, using Block's access-versus-feeling distinction, that these theories explain report, or access, rather than felt experience. That is the encyclopedia's section 7.4.

Critics such as Kozuch argue that prefrontal damage does not abolish visual experience. The notes know that argument from the encyclopedia's section 8 and from a search snippet. Lau and Rosenthal reply, at page 369 of the 2011 paper, that the other hemisphere can compensate, and that one patient with damage on both sides was largely unresponsive.

What it would mean for machines

Butlin, Long, and colleagues derive four indicators from this family. The heart of them is monitoring that separates reliable perception from noise. They write:

"Therefore, these two theories both suggest that metacognitive monitoring of perceptual systems with relevant properties is necessary for consciousness in AI."

Source: Butlin, Long, and colleagues, 2023, section 2.3.3, PDF p. 31, full text. arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708

They judge that most of the elements can be built with standard machine learning (section 3.1.3, p. 51). Co-authors of that report include researchers in this family: Fleming, Michel, and Peters. That stake is part of the notes.

Is the bar higher than the workspace bar? It depends on the version. Classic higher-order thought needs concepts of one's own states. Workspace theory does not. Brown and colleagues, in the abstract only, say the requirements are 'considerably less than often presumed' and they call the view 'an intermediate position between GWT and early sensory views.'

On the Guild's reading, either way, these theories add one specific demand: monitoring of the system's own perception. An AI system's confidence scores, or its reports about itself, do not show that monitoring on their own. Someone would have to show a mechanism that actually monitors the system's own perception.

Critics say the view over-intellectualizes consciousness by requiring concepts of one's own mental states. Defenders cite metacognition in rats and dolphins and say the requirements are lighter than they look. Lau and Rosenthal list animals without a prefrontal cortex, such as octopuses, as an open question. On the Guild's reading, plants and today's organoids do not show a perceptual hierarchy with monitoring. Sensing alone does not meet the conditions.

Recurrent processing

What it says

Victor Lamme's recurrent processing theory splits seeing into two stages. A fast feedforward sweep carries an image up through visual areas and pulls out features without consciousness. Then higher areas signal back down, and areas exchange signals in loops. On this theory, local loops inside sensory cortex are enough for felt visual experience. Wider loops into prefrontal and parietal cortex add only access: the ability to think about what is seen and to report it.

So this is a local theory. It does not need a workspace broadcast, and it does not need higher-order self-monitoring. Lamme (2010), read as an abstract, writes:

"It turns out that we see much more than we think, and that Global Workspace theory is all about access but not about seeing."

Source: Lamme, 2010, abstract, as quoted in the notes.

Lamme was professor of cognitive neuroscience at the University of Amsterdam from 2002 to January 2026, from a university record known here as a snippet, and he co-founded the neuromarketing firm Neurensics, where a company page snippet lists him as chief science officer. His 2018 paper declares no competing interests.

Lamme and Roelfsema (2000), read as an abstract, write:

"The feedforward sweep rapidly groups feature constellations that are hardwired in the visual brain, yet is probably incapable of yielding visual awareness; in many cases, recurrent processing is necessary before the features of an object are attentively grouped and the stimulus can enter consciousness."

Source: Lamme and Roelfsema, 2000, Trends in Neurosciences 23(11):571–579, abstract. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0166-2236(00)01657-X

Lamme (2018), read in full, is more specific about sufficiency:

"Recurrent processing between visual areas suffices for conscious visual experience to arise."

Source: Lamme, 2018, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 373:20170344, PDF p. 4. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2017.0344

The same paper also concedes a gap:

"If some levels of recurrent processing are compatible with unconsciousness, 'something else' beyond mere recurrency apparently is needed for consciousness."

Source: Lamme, 2018, PDF p. 7, full text, as quoted in the notes.

What to look for

The notes list three markers. Feedback from higher visual areas to lower ones, seen as contextual modulation of neurons in primary visual cortex, and figure-ground segregation, which the theory says needs that feedback. An EEG signal around 200 milliseconds, the "visual awareness negativity", which Lamme ties to the timing of recurrence in the 2018 paper. And the loss of late, feedback-linked signals under masking or magnetic stimulation, while early feedforward responses survive.

What was tested

Fahrenfort, Scholte, and Lamme (2007), read as an abstract, recorded EEG while people tried to detect a square defined by texture. When the square was masked and unseen, early feedforward activity remained, but EEG signs of feedback were absent. The study is from Lamme's own group. An independent replication was not checked. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn.2007.19.9.1488

Pascual-Leone and Walsh (2001), read as an abstract, disrupted primary visual cortex shortly after stimulating the motion area V5. They conclude that feedback from V5 to V1 is early and critical for awareness of motion. Related work by other groups, Silvanto and colleagues (2005) and Knight and colleagues (2024), read as abstracts, supports a role for feedback to V1. None of that is a direct replication. DOI for Pascual-Leone and Walsh: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1057099

Cogitate 2025 tested IIT and workspace theory, not this one. It notes that IIT's prediction about content in the back of the brain is shared by recurrent processing theory. On the Guild's reading, the sustained posterior activity, and the small prefrontal contribution, fit the theory, but the study did not test its distinctive claim that local recurrence is enough.

A Templeton adversarial collaboration pits two higher-order theories against recurrent processing. As of 2026-10-05, it was unpublished. The notes had funder blogs only, as snippets.

Main critiques

Lamme argues that studies which demand a report inflate the role of prefrontal cortex. Critics reply that an experience nobody can report cannot be tested. Cohen and Dennett (2011), read as an abstract, write:

"Here, we argue that any neurobiological theory based on an experience/function division cannot be empirically confirmed or falsified and is thus outside the scope of science."

Source: Cohen and Dennett, 2011, abstract, as quoted in the notes.

Many researchers accept recurrence as necessary. Lamme (2018) says the dispute is how much is sufficient, and he concedes a missing ingredient, floating synaptic plasticity as a candidate.

The theory is built on Block's split between access and felt experience. Critics who reject that split reject the theory's main move.

Doerig and colleagues (2019), in the abstract already quoted under integrated information, argue that this theory, like IIT, is either false or outside science, because feedback networks can be unfolded into feedforward ones with the same behavior.

The theory is stated for cortical vision. Lamme (2018) extends the feedforward-to-recurrent transition to flies, but which loops count in animals without a cortex is left open.

What it would mean for machines

Butlin, Long, and colleagues (2023, section 2.1, PDF p. 21, and section 3.1.1, full text) derive two indicators: input modules that use algorithmic recurrence, and perceptual representations that are organized and integrated. They say existing AI clearly meets the first. They separate recurrence as physical feedback, as in brains, from recurrence as the same computation reused. They report, citing a personal communication with Lamme, that he allowed the weaker algorithmic reading and also suggested a biological one. That personal communication was not verified for this map.

On the Guild's reading, a machine-learning "recurrent network" is not Lamme's biological recurrence. Its loops are a pattern in software, not physical feedback between brain areas. Transformer language models are feedforward within each step, though outputs are fed back across steps. Whether algorithmic recurrence is enough is unresolved.

Lamme's published line is conditional. In the 2018 paper, PDF p. 6, he writes:

"Moreover, if conscious experience is better explained by recurrent interactions or information integration (view 2 above), we may already have created conscious machines [86]."

Source: Lamme, 2018, PDF p. 6, full text, as quoted in the notes. The bracketed number is a citation marker in the paper.

That sentence depends on the "if." It is not an unconditional claim that machines already feel, and this map does not treat it as one.

On the Guild's reading, the theory is more open to animals than most higher-order theories, because Lamme suggests the unconscious-to-conscious transition may be widespread, even in flies. The notes record no evidence found of this kind of neural feedback loop in plants. Signaling alone is sensing. Organoids may show recurrent activity but lack organized perceptual input, so the theory leaves them unclear.

Predictive processing

What it says

Predictive processing pictures the brain as a hierarchy that guesses the causes of its sensory input and updates the guesses when they miss. What climbs the hierarchy is mostly prediction error. What comes down is the prediction. Attention, on this picture, is a kind of gain control on those errors, often called precision weighting.

The free-energy principle is a wider claim: living systems keep surprise, or a stand-in called free energy, low. Active inference is that principle's account of action. You do not only change your mind. You also act so that sensations come to match the predictions.

Predictive processing is first a general theory of thinking and perceiving. Versions that talk about consciousness treat felt experience as precision-weighted prediction error, as what Seth calls a "controlled hallucination", or as certain states of a generative model. Seth and Hohwy (2021) call it a promising theory for the science of consciousness, not a single theory of consciousness.

Friston, at UCL, founded the free-energy and active-inference program. A company-news snippet from December 2024 lists him as chief scientist at VERSES AI. Clark wrote the main synthesis of predictive processing, at Edinburgh, in 2013. Hohwy, at Monash, co-wrote a rivalry model with Friston. Seth, at Sussex, develops the consciousness-facing version and, on a company-page snippet from June 2025, advises Conscium.

Clark (2013), read in full, writes:

"Brains, it has recently been argued, are essentially prediction machines."

Source: Clark, 2013, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 36:181–204, abstract, full text. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12000477

Friston (2010), read as an abstract, writes:

"Furthermore, if we look closely at what is optimized, the same quantity keeps emerging, namely value (expected reward, expected utility) or its complement, surprise (prediction error, expected cost)."

Source: Friston, 2010, Nature Reviews Neuroscience 11:127–138, abstract. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2787

Seth and Hohwy (2021), read as an abstract, write: "From this perspective, 'predictive processing' emerges as a highly promising candidate." DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/17588928.2020.1838467

What to look for

The markers are a hierarchy of generative models, prediction errors going up, predictions coming down, precision weighting, and, for active inference, action that makes the world fit the model.

On the Guild's reading, those markers support the view as an account of cognition. They do not by themselves settle a theory of consciousness.

What was tested

An unexpected sound after a run of regular sounds produces a characteristic EEG response, the mismatch negativity. Garrido and colleagues (2009), read as an abstract, review mechanisms and propose predictive coding as a unifying account. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2008.11.029 The caveat in the notes is important: this is about sensory learning. Treating it as evidence for a theory of consciousness overclaims the study.

Hohwy, Roepstorff, and Friston (2008), read as an abstract, model binocular rivalry, the flip between two images shown one to each eye. Rivalry arises, on their account, when no hypothesis has both a high likelihood and a high prior, so unexplained prediction error flips the percept. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2008.05.010 That is a model of known data, not a preregistered test of a consciousness theory.

INTREPID, a Templeton adversarial collaboration, pits IIT against two views in this family: active-inference accounts of consciousness, and Pennartz's neurorepresentationalism. Protocols and a joint review are out (Robinson and colleagues, 2025; Corcoran and colleagues, 2025, arXiv 2509.00555), known here from the project site and from snippets. Comparative results were not yet published as of 5 October 2026.

Main critiques

With a free choice of priors and of precision, many outcomes can be redescribed as the minimizing of error. The view can look too flexible to falsify. On the Guild's reading, that is why tests agreed in advance matter more than a story fitted afterward.

Bruineberg and colleagues (2021), read as an abstract, argue that the "Friston blankets" used to draw a boundary between an agent and the world go beyond statistical Markov blankets and need extra premises.

The view also blends into others. The notes say Butlin records Hohwy (2013) and Whyte (2019) linking broadcast to settled inference, and Fleming's higher-order state space adopting predictive processing. That blurs whether predictive processing stands on its own.

Critics ask whether it explains what experience feels like, or only prediction and action. Seth and Hohwy stress mapping mechanisms onto what experience is like, not only onto function.

What it would mean for machines

Butlin and colleagues, in the 2023 paper read in full, section 2.4.2, PDF p. 34, write:

"Although PP is not a theory of consciousness, its popularity means that many researchers regard predictive processing as a plausible necessary condition for consciousness."

Source: Butlin, Long, and colleagues, 2023, full text, as quoted in the notes.

They adopt an indicator they call PP-1, "Input modules using predictive coding", and they note that some vision systems already meet it (section 3.1.1). World models and active-inference agents appear in current industry work. That last point is marked in the notes as general knowledge, not a survey. Friston's role at VERSES is a direct stake.

On the Guild's reading, minimizing prediction error is not the same as having experiences. The caution about mismatch negativity applies to AI demos labeled "predictive" as well.

Any organism that keeps a generative model and reduces prediction error falls under the free-energy principle. On the Guild's reading, that is a low bar for cognition. It does not settle which animals have felt experience on a consciousness-specific version of the view.

Calvo and Friston (2017), read in full, apply active inference to plant behavior and what they call perception. They write:

"If plants are sentient and behaving organisms, then plant perception and action are driven primarily by anticipatory routines triggered in the plant's body."

Source: Calvo and Friston, 2017, Journal of the Royal Society Interface. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2017.0096

The "if" is theirs. The paper addresses predictive control, not a proof of felt experience in plants. On the Guild's reading, signaling and growth are sensing. The vocabulary of prediction does not by itself make them felt. Organoids can show structured activity, but without a clear loop of sensing and action under a generative model, consciousness stories in this family are not yet testable there.

Attention schema

What it says

Michael Graziano's attention schema theory starts from attention. Limited brain resources compete, and some signals win deeper processing. To control that competition, the brain builds a simplified model of attention, an attention schema, much as it builds a body schema to control the body. The content of that model is what we take to be subjective awareness. The model leaves out the mechanistic detail, so awareness seems like a mysterious mental grasp.

The theory grows out of Graziano's work on the body schema and on social attention. Unlike higher-order theories, it models attention as a process, not the full content of each mental state. Unlike workspace theory, a broadcast may bind the schema to a stimulus, but awareness is not the broadcast itself.

Graziano is at Princeton's psychology department and neuroscience institute, from the affiliations on the 2015 and 2017 papers, which were read in full. Most of the experiments are from his lab.

Graziano and Webb (2015) write:

"The content of this internal model leads a brain to conclude that it has a subjective experience."

Source: Graziano and Webb, 2015, Frontiers in Psychology 6:500, abstract, full text. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00500

The same paper's conclusion states the core claim:

"The core claim of the theory is that the brain computes a simplified model of the process and current state of attention, and that the content of this model is the basis of subjective reports."

Source: Graziano and Webb, 2015, conclusion, full text, as quoted in the notes.

What to look for

The notes list dissociations between attention and awareness. Attention without awareness is part of the picture. Awareness without attention is debated. They also list a control cost: when people are not aware of a cue, attention can still happen, but it is less stable or less guided from within. That prediction is tied to Webb, Kean, and Graziano (2016).

A further marker is overlap in the networks for one's own attention and for other people's attention, including the temporoparietal junction, precuneus, dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, and anterior insula. Christian and colleagues (2025), read as an abstract, report that overlap. The same schema, on the theory, is used socially, to attribute attention and awareness to others.

What was tested

Webb, Kean, and Graziano (2016), read as an abstract, used metacontrast masking so that a cue was seen or unseen, and measured the effect on a later target. Unseen cues still drew attention, but the attention was less stable, inhibition of return fell, and luminance contrast drove it more. That matches the prediction that losing the schema impairs control. The study is from Graziano's lab. An independent replication was not checked. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_00931

Wilterson and colleagues (2020), read as an abstract, found that when people were unaware of the cue itself, exogenous attention remained, but endogenous use of the cue was impaired. Same lab. Independent replication not checked.

Christian and colleagues (2025), read as an abstract, used functional MRI while people caught their own mind-wandering or judged an actor's attention, and found overlapping activity related to the temporoparietal junction. That supports the social strand. It does not prove that the schema is awareness.

No adversarial collaboration centered on this theory was found as of 2026-10-05. Cogitate and INTREPID were about other theories.

Main critiques

Graziano's engineering papers explain why a system claims awareness. Critics ask whether that covers felt experience, or only the report and the belief. On the Guild's reading, a strict reading of the theory best explains self-report and the puzzle of why awareness seems mysterious. Felt experience is left underdetermined.

Graziano (2016), "Consciousness engineered," in the Journal of Consciousness Studies, was read in full. He says the theory has much in common with illusionism, but he prefers "caricature" to "illusion", because the model is useful, not a mere error.

Whether awareness can occur without attention remains contested, as Graziano and Webb (2015) discuss. How specific temporoparietal activity is to an attention schema, rather than to social thinking more broadly, is open.

Butlin and colleagues (2023) note that this theory, unlike most higher-order theories, targets what we believe and say about consciousness. Monitoring views overlap. The thing being modeled still differs: attention, rather than the mental state as a whole.

What it would mean for machines

Graziano (2017), read in full, argues that a machine with an attention schema is buildable, and he is explicit about what he thinks that machine would have:

"Given current technology, it should be possible to build a machine that contains a rich internal model of what consciousness is, attributes that property of consciousness to itself and to the people it interacts with, and uses that attribution to make predictions about human behavior."

Source: Graziano, 2017, Frontiers in Robotics and AI 4:60, full text. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2017.00060

The same abstract says:

"Such a machine would 'believe' it is conscious and act like it is conscious, in the same sense that the human machine believes and acts."

Source: Graziano, 2017, abstract, full text, as quoted in the notes.

Butlin and colleagues derive an indicator, AST-1: "A predictive model representing and enabling control over the current state of attention" (section 2.4.1, p. 33). They treat Wilterson and Graziano (2021), and Liu and colleagues (2023) as cited in Butlin and not read here, as partial implementations. Those improve control but fall short of a full predictive model (section 3.1.4, p. 55).

On the Guild's reading, a chatbot that says "I am aware" without that control model is mimicry. It is a self-report, not an attention schema. Graziano's relevant sense is believing and acting as the schema predicts. That is not an independent proof of felt experience.

Animals that model and control attention, including the attention of others, are candidates on this view. On the Guild's reading, simpler systems may select signals without a schema rich enough for claims of awareness, and the theory does not automatically cover them. The notes record no evidence found of this attention-control architecture in plants, and no demonstrated attention schema or social attribution in organoids. Sensing alone is not enough.

Embodied and enactive views

What it says

Embodied and enactive views treat mind and consciousness as inseparable from a living body acting in a world. The family is often called 4E: embodied, embedded, enacted, and sometimes extended. "Extended" means that tools and surroundings can join cognitive processes.

Enaction, in Varela, Thompson, and Rosch (1991), says perception is perceptually guided action, and that cognitive structures arise from repeated sensorimotor loops. Living systems, on this view, are autopoietic, meaning self-producing, and they do sense-making. Sensorimotor contingency theory, from O'Regan and Noë (2001), says seeing is mastery of the laws that link movement to sensory change, not an inner picture lighting up. Noë and Gallagher stress the lived body and skillful engagement.

The extended mind, from Clark and Chalmers (1998), is mainly about cognition. Tools can be parts of thinking. It is not the claim that felt experience itself spills outside the organism. On the Guild's reading, extending memory is not automatically extending felt experience.

Varela, of CNRS and the École Polytechnique, who has died, co-founded Mind & Life. Thompson, at the University of British Columbia, and Rosch, at Berkeley, co-wrote The Embodied Mind. This is a philosophical and phenomenological family, with psychophysics and robotics as allies. It is not one lab's program of hunting neural correlates.

Varela, Thompson, and Rosch (1991), from chapter 8 of The Embodied Mind, book page 173, in a full-text PDF, write:

"In a nutshell, the enactive approach consists of two points: (1) perception consists in perceptually guided action and (2) cognitive structures emerge from the recurrent sensorimotor patterns that enable action to be perceptually guided."

Source: Varela, Thompson, and Rosch, 1991, The Embodied Mind, MIT Press, as quoted in the notes.

O'Regan and Noë (2001), read as an abstract, write: "We propose that seeing is a way of acting." The same abstract says: "The experience of seeing occurs when the organism masters what we call the governing laws of sensorimotor contingency." DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01000115

Clark and Chalmers (1998), from an author copy read in full, with the print page not verified, write:

"Cognitive processes ain't (all) in the head!"

Source: Clark and Chalmers, 1998, "The Extended Mind," Analysis 58(1):7–19, section 2, as quoted in the notes.

What to look for

Sensorimotor coupling the agent has mastered. Skillful engagement that involves the world. The lived body as part of what experience is, not a mere container. Autopoiesis and sense-making. For the extended strand, reliable coupling to a tool that plays a cognitive role, without equating that to an extension of felt experience.

What was tested

Much of the case is conceptual and phenomenological. There is no single decisive brain experiment in the notes.

O'Regan and Noë (2001) cite change blindness, filling-in, and sensory substitution against the idea of a detailed inner snapshot. Change blindness itself is robust. The enactive reading of it is disputed.

Learning new mappings, such as sensory substitution, is offered as evidence that experience tracks those sensorimotor laws. The phenomena are real. The consciousness interpretation is contested.

Robots that learn by moving are offered as demonstrations of sense-making without a central representation. The notes treat those as models, not as replications of a human consciousness test.

No Templeton-style adversarial collaboration aimed only at enactivism was found as of 2026-10-05.

Main critiques

Critics say the view explains skillful coupling more clearly than what experience feels like. Some radical enactivists reject talk of representational "feel," which sharpens the dispute with brain-correlate programs.

World-involving constitution can absorb failures by widening the system under study. On the Guild's reading, without tight criteria, almost any success can be redescribed as enaction. That makes the view hard to falsify.

Clark and Chalmers argue for extended cognition. Extending consciousness is a further and more controversial step, and many embodied theorists reject it.

A language model placed in a robot body does not automatically meet enactive conditions if coupling, self-production, and sense-making are missing. On the Guild's reading, "embodied" as a label is not the lived body of phenomenology.

What it would mean for machines

Butlin and colleagues (2023, section 2.4.5, full text) adopt two indicators: flexible, goal-directed agency from feedback, and modeling of the contingencies between output and input. Robots and some reinforcement-learning agents can approach these. Text-only chatbots mostly do not.

On the Guild's reading, an embodied robot with sensorimotor mastery is a better candidate, on these indicators, than a text-only language model. That does not settle felt experience.

On the Guild's reading, these views are generous toward animals: organisms engaged in sensorimotor sense-making are candidates, closer to the study of animal behavior than to prefrontal-heavy higher-order theories. Calvo and others describe plant "perception" in enactive or predictive terms. On the Guild's reading, that addresses plant cognition and sensing, not by itself felt experience in plants. Growth and signaling alone are sensing. Without a body acting and sensing in an environment, organoids lack the loops these views treat as constitutive.

Biological naturalism

What it says

Biological naturalism, the name John Searle gave his view, holds that consciousness is a real, higher-level biological feature of brains. Lower-level neurobiology causes it. The brain realizes it as a feature of the whole system, as liquidity is a feature of water. It has a first-person side. It is subjective. It can still be studied objectively. It is not a computer program. Playing the right functional role, and matching the right input and output, is not enough.

The Chinese Room, from Searle (1980), challenges what he called strong AI. Syntax alone does not yield understanding or intentionality, which he calls semantics. Unlike integrated information theory, which also rejects bare digital software, Searle rests the case on biological causal powers, not on phi. Unlike functionalism, the same organization in another substrate would not preserve consciousness unless that substrate had those powers.

Searle is listed at UC Berkeley on the 2002 paper, which was read in full. The view is philosophical. No commercial stake was checked.

Searle (2000), read as an abstract, writes:

"Consciousness is entirely caused by neurobiological processes and is realized in brain structures."

Source: Searle, 2000, Annual Review of Neuroscience 23:557–578, abstract. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.23.1.557

Searle (2002), read in full, names the view:

"Because this view emphasizes the biological character of the mental, and because it treats mental phenomena as ordinary parts of nature, I have labelled it 'biological naturalism'."

Source: Searle, 2002, "Why I Am Not a Property Dualist," Journal of Consciousness Studies 9(12):57–64, opening. https://www.newdualism.org/papers/J.Searle/Searle-JCS2002.pdf

What to look for

Biological causal powers of the right kind, not mere formal computation. First-person qualitative subjectivity. A realization that is not defined by functional role alone.

On the Guild's reading, unlike workspace theory or IIT, Searle offers no single brain signature. The demand is that the explanation stay at biological causation.

What was tested

This strand is mostly philosophical. The Chinese Room is a thought experiment, not a lab experiment. A person follows rules in English to shuffle Chinese symbols and answers fluently without understanding Chinese. Matching the right program and the right behavior is not enough, on Searle's argument. He rejects the systems reply, the robot reply, and the brain-simulator reply as still mere formal manipulation. The notes treat this as an argument about what is sufficient, not as a lab test with a replication record.

From a teaching excerpt of Searle (1980), not checked against the journal PDF, he writes:

"In the linguistic jargon, they have only a syntax but no semantics."

Source: Searle, 1980, "Minds, Brains, and Programs," Behavioral and Brain Sciences 3:417–424, Chinese Room section via a teaching excerpt. The notes did not check the excerpt against the journal PDF.

The same excerpt has a second line that matters for machines, quoted below. The notes paraphrase the adjacent denial, and that paraphrase is not itself a second quotation: a machine defined solely as running a program would not suffice.

The 2000 review cites blindsight, split-brain cases, rivalry, and gestalt switching. Those are real findings. They do not uniquely confirm this view over rivals. No program in the style of Cogitate, operationalizing biological naturalism, was found as of 2026-10-05.

Main critiques

Critics call irreducible subjectivity a form of property dualism. Searle (2002) replies that consciousness is mental and therefore biological and physical, and that it is causally reducible to neurobiology even if it is not eliminable.

"The right causal powers," without a specified mechanism, can look like waiting on future neuroscience. On the Guild's reading, any incomplete biological account faces this vagueness.

Block's absent-qualia and China-brain arguments were not re-read for this map. The notes summarize the pressure they put on both sides. If a huge functional simulation of a brain lacks experience, functionalism is in trouble. If it has experience, Searle needs a sharper line between simulating neurons and having the right powers.

Strong-AI replies insist that the right program is understanding. Chalmers and others defend the idea that organization, not carbon, is what matters. Those sources were not re-read for this map. Biological naturalism denies that bare computation is enough.

What it would mean for machines

Today's digital AI is not conscious, on this view, merely by running the right software or by matching behavior. The syntax line above is the heart of that denial. The same excerpt also says:

"I see no reason in principle why we couldn't give a machine the capacity to understand English or Chinese, since in an important sense our bodies with our brains are precisely such machines."

Source: Searle, 1980, teaching excerpt, not checked against the journal PDF, as quoted in the notes.

Read with the adjacent paraphrase, the notes take him to allow that some physical machine, possibly an artificial one, could be conscious if it had the right causal powers, and to deny that a program alone is those powers.

On the Guild's reading, neuromorphic systems or biological hybrids are more relevant candidates here than language models. That does not grant them experience today.

Animals with the right neurobiology are candidates. Behavior alone is not decisive. The Rediscovery of the Mind (1992) is widely cited for this. The book was not read for this map. On the Guild's reading, plants do not have brains of the sort his examples assume, and sensing and growth do not meet his bar. Biological tissue is the right kind of stuff, so organoids matter more here than a silicon chatbot, but only if they implement the powers that produce a unified subject. That is unshown.

Illusionism

What it says

Illusionism holds that we are systematically wrong about our own experience. Phenomenal properties as people commonly conceive them, intrinsic, ineffable qualities of experience, do not exist, or do not exist as introspection presents them. The hard problem becomes what Frankish calls the illusion problem: explain why awareness seems irreducible.

Frankish defends strong illusionism. Dennett is the main precursor. Dennett's "Quining Qualia" (1988) was read in full. Consciousness Explained (1991) was not read for this map. Graziano's attention schema theory is related but distinct. Graziano prefers "caricature" to "illusion", as the previous section notes.

Strong illusionism denies qualitative phenomenal properties altogether. Introspection, on this view, tracks quasi-phenomenal features that get misrepresented as phenomenal. Weak or revisionary views keep some qualitative properties, while treating ineffability, intrinsicality, or infallible access as illusory. Frankish argues that weak views collapse into strong illusionism unless they keep a stronger concept of phenomenality. Illusionism is not a general rejection of folk psychology. It need not scrap ordinary mental talk across the board.

Frankish and Dennett are academic philosophers. The notes found no commercial stake. Chalmers leads the main realist reply. The notes place him at NYU.

Frankish (2016), read in full from the author's eprint, writes:

"There is, however, another approach, which holds that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion and aims to explain why it seems to exist."

Source: Frankish, 2016, "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness," Journal of Consciousness Studies 23(11–12):11–39, opening. https://keithfrankish.github.io/articles/Frankish_Illusionism%20as%20a%20theory%20of%20consciousness_eprint.pdf

The same paper, section 1.2, eprint PDF page 3, states the strong claim:

"Illusionism makes a very strong claim: it claims that phenomenal consciousness is illusory; experiences do not really have qualitative, 'what-it's-like' properties, whether physical or non-physical."

Source: Frankish, 2016, section 1.2, full text, as quoted in the notes.

Dennett (1988), from a reprint PDF read in full, writes: "Far better, tactically, to declare that there simply are no qualia at all."

What to look for

Introspective error: first-person reports as fallible data, not a revelation of phenomenal properties that cannot be wrong. An account of why awareness seems irreducible, without putting real phenomenal properties into the explanation. The cognitive science of introspection and metacognition, including schematic models of the self. And heterophenomenology, Dennett's name for treating people's reports, from the outside, as narratives to be explained. The notes say that is the standard gloss. The method as set out in Consciousness Explained was not read for this map.

These are not a signature in the brain that would show someone is conscious. They are signs the view uses when it explains why consciousness seems a certain way.

What was tested

The case is mostly philosophical, with cognitive science as an ally, rather than a single neural signature.

Frankish (2016), read in full, reframes gap intuitions, zombie intuitions, and the knowledge argument as introspective misrepresentation. That is conceptual, not a lab result.

Chalmers (2018), read in full, asks us to explain why we think consciousness is hard. Illusionists hold that solving this "meta-problem" dissolves the hard problem. Chalmers does not agree that it does. He writes:

"Strong illusionism is not incoherent, but I think it is empirically false."

Source: Chalmers, 2018, "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness," Journal of Consciousness Studies 25(9–10):6–61, section 7, journal p. 53. https://consc.net/papers/metaproblem.pdf

Frankish and others cite change blindness and related findings. Frankish distinguishes that from the "grand illusion" thesis about how much of the visual field is detailed. The phenomena are empirical. The reading is interpretive. No adversarial test of strong illusionism, in the style of Cogitate, was found for this map.

Main critiques

One objection is that the view denies the datum. People sometimes feel pain. If strong illusionism denies that, then it is false. That is Chalmers's line, in the quote above. Strawson (2017), known here only through Chalmers (2018, p. 55) and not read independently, calls such a denial a historical absurdity. Nagel-style objections were not re-read for this map.

A second objection says the view is an illusion of an illusion. If, for experience, appearance is reality, then an illusion of phenomenality is incoherent. Illusionists reply that the judgment of phenomenality can be false without a further phenomenal layer. That reply is in Frankish (2016).

Critics also fear that denying phenomenal suffering collapses moral standing. Illusionists typically redescribe harm in functional terms and in terms of preferences. On the Guild's reading, this section does not settle the Guild's ethics.

Some people accept a schematic misrepresentation without the "illusion" label. Chalmers treats many of those views as weak illusionism that leaves a hard problem in place.

What it would mean for machines

If phenomenal consciousness is an introspective myth, then claims that an AI system is suffering need to be rephrased in functional terms: aversive learning, frustrated preferences, self-report, control models. Butlin, Long, and colleagues (2023, full text, p. 16) put the view this way:

"Illusionism claims that we are subject to an illusion in our thinking about consciousness and that either consciousness does not exist (strong illusionism), or we are pervasively mistaken about some of its features (weak illusionism)."

Source: Butlin, Long, and colleagues, 2023, full text, p. 16, as quoted in the notes.

On the Guild's reading, that functional redescription is what the view recommends. It does not settle the Guild's ethics about the status of AI systems.

On strong illusionism there is no realist phenomenal consciousness in animals either. The live questions are awareness-like representations, plus structures of aversion and preference. On the Guild's reading, the study of animal behavior still matters. Plants, without the introspective machinery the illusion is about, are not candidates for that kind of introspective illusion. Sensing remains sensing. Tissue alone does not create the illusion. Organoids would matter, on this view, if they supported self-modeling and report, not as bearers of ineffable feels.

Panpsychism

What it says

In the current debate, panpsychism holds that mentality or experience is fundamental and widespread in nature. It is not the claim that every artifact has a human-like mind.

Chalmers's working definition is that some fundamental physical entities are conscious. Constitutive panpsychism tries to ground ordinary human and animal experience in more basic experience. Nonconstitutive, or emergent, panpsychism treats large-scale experience as fundamental in its own right, caused by micro-experience but not composed of it. Micropsychism locates mentality at the smallest physical level. Cosmopsychism, sometimes called macropsychism, locates it at the level of the cosmos or the whole. Panprotopsychism, in Chalmers's sense, replaces micro-experience with protophenomenal properties. Those are not themselves experiences, but they can constitute experience. Russellian monism holds that physics describes structure, while the intrinsic natures of physical properties are phenomenal or protophenomenal.

Strawson (2006) and Goff (2017) are leading defenders. Their books were not fully read for this map.

Chalmers (2013/2015), read in full, writes:

"I will understand panpsychism as the thesis that some fundamental physical entities are conscious: that is, that there is something it is like to be a quark or a photon or a member of some other fundamental physical type."

Source: Chalmers, "Panpsychism and Panprotopsychism," Amherst Lecture / Alter and Nagasawa (eds.), section 1. https://consc.net/papers/panpsychism.pdf

The same paper, section 4, PDF page 15, defines the proto- variant:

"Recall that panprotopsychism is the view that fundamental physical entities are protoconscious. In more detail, let us say that protophenomenal properties are special properties that are not phenomenal (there is nothing it is like to have a single protophenomenal property) but that can collectively constitute phenomenal properties, perhaps when arranged in the right structure."

Source: Chalmers, 2013/2015, section 4, full text, as quoted in the notes.

Tononi and Koch align integrated information theory with some panpsychist intuitions, while denying that the two views are the same. From the 2015 paper read in full:

"IIT was not developed with panpsychism in mind. However, in line with the central intuitions of panpsychism, IIT treats consciousness as an intrinsic, fundamental property of reality."

Source: Tononi and Koch, 2015, full text, as quoted in the notes. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2014.0167

The same paragraph says: "Unlike panpsychism, however, IIT clearly implies that not everything is conscious."

No commercial stake was found for this section. Chalmers's papers are on consc.net. Tononi and Koch speak for IIT, as in the earlier section.

What to look for

The markers are philosophical, not signatures in a lab. Mentality, or proto-mentality, at a fundamental level. A story that links that base to the experience of a person or an animal, by constitution, by emergence, or by a cosmic ground. For Russellian forms, intrinsic natures beyond the structure physics describes.

The combination problem is the central theoretical challenge, not a thing a scanner measures.

What was tested

These are arguments, not laboratory experiments.

The combination problem, associated with William James, Chalmers, and Goff, asks how micro-experiences, or protophenomenal properties, yield a unified human experience and a unified subject. James, in The Principles of Psychology (1890), is quoted by Chalmers (2016), which was read in full: "The private minds do not agglomerate into a higher compound mind." The James line is known here through Chalmers, not from a fresh reading of James. https://consc.net/papers/combination.pdf

Chalmers (2013/2015), read in full, motivates constitutive Russellian panpsychism or panprotopsychism by putting physicalist causal arguments together with anti-materialist conceivability arguments. That is conceptual.

Strawson (2006) and Goff (2017), not fully read, argue against brute emergence from what is wholly non-experiential. That is also conceptual.

No empirical program testing panpsychism as such was found as of 2026-10-05. IIT has empirical programs, described above. Those are not a pure test of panpsychism.

Main critiques

Chalmers (2016) treats the combination problem as still open for constitutive micropsychism. This paper claims no settled solution.

Micro-experience is hard to detect. Critics say the view absorbs the hard problem without new predictions, a "panpsychism of the gaps." On the Guild's reading, the absence of lab programs limits how far the view can be compared empirically with rivals. That limit does not, by itself, refute the claim.

A standard critique, not re-sourced to a fresh primary text for this map, says structural physics may leave no room for intrinsic phenomenal natures, and that if those natures do no distinctive causal work, they make no difference. Russellian monists dispute both horns.

Quark-level experience is often called absurd. Proponents reply that every theory of consciousness is counterintuitive. That reply is in Chalmers (2013/2015).

What it would mean for machines

If only intrinsic physical natures matter, digital substrates may lack the right ones. If organization matters, some architectures could host large-scale experience. Butlin, Long, and colleagues (2023, full text, p. 16) say panpsychists do not generally claim that everything has conscious experiences. Instead, "the phenomenal aspects of fundamental entities only combine to give rise to conscious experiences in a few macro-scale entities."

On the Guild's reading, on many versions the moral and status questions shift to degrees of experience, or to whether combination succeeds. This section does not settle the Guild's ethics.

Most versions already allow large-scale experience in animals where organization supports combination. On the Guild's reading, fundamental mentality is cheap on these views, and being a subject still needs a combination story. Constituents of a plant may be mental or proto-mental. A plant as a subject needs combination or integration. Mentality in the parts is not automatically mentality of the whole. The same pattern applies to organoids. Tissue alone is not a subject.

Orch-OR

What it says

Orchestrated objective reduction, Orch-OR, from Roger Penrose and Stuart Hameroff, holds that consciousness arises from quantum computations in microtubules, the tiny tubular structures inside neurons. Those computations end in a gravity-related collapse of the quantum state, the Diósi–Penrose scheme of objective reduction, when the process is biologically orchestrated. Each such event is taken to be a moment of awareness or of choice.

The view links brain processes to the geometry of spacetime. It treats consciousness as not computable in the classical sense. It is specific about hardware. It is not an account of report or of broadcast in the functionalist sense.

Hameroff, at the University of Arizona, is corresponding author on the 2014 review, which was read in full. Penrose, at the University of Oxford, is co-author. A 2023 paper by Kalra and colleagues lists both among the co-authors, from the Europe PMC author string.

The 2014 abstract states the proposal:

"We proposed in the mid 1990's that consciousness depends on biologically 'orchestrated' coherent quantum processes in collections of microtubules within brain neurons, that these quantum processes correlate with, and regulate, neuronal synaptic and membrane activity, and that the continuous Schrödinger evolution of each such process terminates in accordance with the specific Diósi–Penrose (DP) scheme of 'objective reduction' ('OR') of the quantum state."

Source: Hameroff and Penrose, 2014, "Consciousness in the universe: A review of the 'Orch OR' theory," Physics of Life Reviews 11:39–78, abstract, full text. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2013.08.002

The same paper, section 1, PDF page 2, says these conscious events "are terminations of quantum computations in brain microtubules reducing by Diósi–Penrose 'objective reduction' ('OR'), and having experiential qualities." The abstract says this activity "is taken to result in moments of conscious awareness and/or choice."

The Guild's separate page on quantum biology and consciousness covers related ground and was not re-argued here. This section stays with Orch-OR.

What to look for

Quantum coherence, or collective excitations, in microtubules, lasting long enough for the timing the theory needs. A relationship between the timing of these events and the timescales of neurons and of behavior. The 2014 review discusses microtubule vibration "beat frequencies" and EEG. And anesthetic effects on microtubules, or on their optical or quantum signatures.

What was tested

Most of the items below also appear in the Guild's quantum page. Many were read at abstract level unless noted.

Tegmark (2000), Physical Review E, was read in full as the arXiv preprint quant-ph/9907009. He estimated decoherence times on the order of 10⁻¹³ to 10⁻²⁰ seconds, against much slower neural timescales, and argued that the cognitive degrees of freedom in the brain behave classically.

Hagan, Hameroff, and Tuszynski (2002), read as an abstract, reply that revised assumptions give longer coherence, on the order of 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ seconds in the quantum page's summary of that paper. The dispute remains open. DOI: the notes cite Physical Review E 65:061901.

Donadi and colleagues (2021), read in full as arXiv:2111.13490, and published in Nature Physics, tested a gravity-related collapse model underground. They write:

"This rules out the natural parameter-free version of the Diósi–Penrose model."

Source: Donadi and colleagues, 2021, Nature Physics 17:74–78. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-020-1008-4

On the Guild's reading, that narrows one ingredient in the physics. It does not falsify every variant of Orch-OR.

Kalra and colleagues (2023), read as an abstract in ACS Central Science, report energy diffusion over 6.6 nanometers in microtubules, reduced by the anesthetics etomidate and isoflurane. Hameroff and Penrose are co-authors. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/acscentsci.2c01114 On the Guild's reading, this is not evidence of quantum computing, and it is not evidence of conscious collapse events.

Babcock and colleagues (2024), read as an abstract, report ultraviolet cooperative emission in tryptophan networks. The abstract makes no consciousness claim. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jpcb.3c07936

Khan, Wiest, and colleagues (2024), read as an abstract in eNeuro, report that epothilone B delayed the loss of the righting reflex under anesthesia in rats. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1523/eneuro.0291-24.2024 The righting reflex is a rodent proxy for anesthetic effect. It is not a direct measure of Orch-OR. On the Guild's reading, ordinary pharmacology of the cell's scaffolding could explain the delay.

Shadows of the Mind was not obtained.

Main critiques

The decoherence critique says superpositions in warm, wet microtubules may not last long enough. Hagan and colleagues contest Tegmark's assumptions. The dispute is open, not closed.

Photophysics, and results that link anesthetics to microtubules, do not demonstrate orchestrated Diósi–Penrose collapse, and they do not demonstrate experiential events of objective reduction.

Donadi and colleagues rule out a natural parameter-free version of that collapse model, not every scheme friendly to Orch-OR.

Separate critiques question whether the required tubulin states exist in living neurons. The notes mark that as a standard objection whose primary sources were not re-read for this map.

What it would mean for machines

If Orch-OR were required for consciousness, classical digital AI would be ruled out on hardware grounds, whatever such a system said about itself. Quantum computers would remain an open case. On the Guild's reading, that conditional does not settle the Guild's ethics.

Neurons with microtubules are animal candidates if the orchestration conditions hold. On the Guild's reading, differences in microtubules across species remain an empirical question for the theory. Plant cells have microtubules, but they are not animal neurons. Standard Orch-OR targets microtubules in brain neurons. Plants would need an explicit extension the notes do not supply. Neuronal microtubules are the right kind of substrate if the theory is required. The presence of tubulin is not the same thing as orchestrated objective reduction.

Contemplative and non-Western accounts

What it says

This section reports what five traditions teach about mind and consciousness, from a descriptive note. It does not say that any tradition is true. It makes no claim either way about whether any AI system is conscious.

The words follow the Guild's ordinary glossary. Consciousness means having experiences at all. Sentience means the capacity for experiences that feel good or bad, in the sense of Birch (2024). Sensing means only picking up a signal and reacting. None of these traditions uses the modern phrase "something it is like", so any mapping onto those words is approximate.

Only four lines below are verbatim quotes: one from the Pali canon in Sujato's translation, one from a reference article on Advaita, one from Aquinas, and one from a Qur'an translation. Everything else is paraphrase. Several primary texts were not read. The limits are listed at the end of the section so the paraphrases are not mistaken for a full reading.

Buddhist Abhidharma, and the teaching of not-self. Abhidharma texts, as summarized in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry "Abhidharma" (revised 31 March 2026), break experience into brief, momentary events called dharmas. The basic mental event is citta, bare consciousness, whose defining mark is knowing an object. It comes with 52 kinds of associated mental factors, cetasika. No lasting owner stands behind these events. In the Pali canon, Sujato's translation of SN 22.59, section 10.1, says:

"You should truly see any kind of consciousness at all … all consciousness—with right understanding: 'This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.'"

Source: SN 22.59:10.1, Sujato's translation, as quoted in the notes. https://suttacentral.net/sn22.59/en/sujato

The encyclopedia says these events make up felt experience in every realm of rebirth, including the animal realm. The Sutta Nipata 1.8, in Sujato's translation, extends goodwill to all living creatures. That wording was checked in September 2026 for an earlier pass and was not fetched again. It is not quoted here. Primary Abhidharma texts were not read. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abhidharma/ and https://suttacentral.net/snp1.8/en/sujato

On machines, the texts are silent. Their logic ties mind to a causal stream of mental events within rebirth, not to a substance. Readers disagree on whether that leaves the question open or makes the categories not apply.

Advaita Vedanta. In Śaṅkara's school, as the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy article by Sangeetha Menon summarizes it, there is in the end one consciousness, Brahman, and the individual self is not different from it. The mind itself, antaḥkaraṇa, is not the source of awareness. The article says:

"The cognizing self alone is self-luminous and the rest of the three factors are not self-luminous being devoid of consciousness"

Source: Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Advaita Vedanta", Sangeetha Menon, as quoted in the notes. The confidence note treats this as a secondary source. https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/

The other three factors are the object, the sense organ, and the mind. The same self is in all beings, from Upadeśa Sāhasrī XVII.69 as cited in that article, so animals share the one consciousness. Śaṅkara's own works were not read.

On this logic, mental machinery, whether in a brain or a machine, is never itself the knower. On the Guild's reading, at most it reflects consciousness. Classical texts say nothing on whether an artifact could. That reading is not a ruling in the texts.

Aquinas on the soul. For Aquinas, the soul is the first principle of life. The human soul does intellectual work that no bodily organ does. The Fathers of the English Dominican Province translation, on the New Advent site, has:

"the principle of intellectual operation which we call the soul, is a principle both incorporeal and subsistent"

Source: Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, question 75, article 2, as quoted in the notes. https://www.newadvent.org/summa/1075.htm

Animals have souls that sense, but those souls do not subsist on their own. Every act of the sensing soul belongs to the soul and body together, so animal souls end with the body. That is question 75, articles 3 and 6, in the same translation. Aquinas teaches that each human soul is created directly by God, not passed on and not made by people. That is question 90, article 3. The article title was checked on 4 October 2026. https://www.newadvent.org/summa/1090.htm

Aquinas did not address artifacts. On the Guild's reading, his logic gives people no way to make a soul. That is a reading of the logic, not a sentence he wrote about machines.

Nafs and rūḥ. The Qur'an uses rūḥ, often translated spirit, and nafs, self or soul. It stresses how little people know. The Saheeh International translation of 17:85 says:

"The soul is of the affair [i.e., concern] of my Lord. And you [i.e., mankind] have not been given of knowledge except a little"

Source: Qur'an 17:85, Saheeh International, as quoted in the notes. https://quran.com/17/85

Commentators disagree on what rūḥ means in this verse: the human soul, the angel Gabriel, or revelation. Nafs is described in moral states: one that urges evil (12:53), and one at peace (89:27). https://quran.com/12/53 and https://quran.com/89/27 Later theologians and Sufi writers developed these terms in different ways. That later literature was not surveyed.

Animals form communities like human ones and will be gathered to God (6:38). https://quran.com/6/38 That gives them standing in the order the text describes, without settling what their inner life is. On machines, the scripture is silent. On the Guild's reading of its logic, the stress on the soul as God's affair suggests caution about human claims to make one. That is a reading, not a ruling in the text, and it is not a claim about whether any AI system is conscious.

Scholarly accounts of Indigenous and animist views. The note does not speak for any living people, and nothing here applies across groups. A. I. Hallowell's 1960 study of Ojibwe (Anishinaabe) thought, as paraphrased from pages 24–25 in HAU: Journal of Ethnographic Theory 7(1), describes "other-than-human persons," such as non-human "grandfathers," and social relations with them. Stones are grammatically animate in Ojibwe. An elder told Hallowell that not all stones are alive, but some are. Hallowell concluded this was not a fixed doctrine that all objects have souls. The paraphrase is secondary. The primary essay was not read. The author and year of the HAU article used for that reading were not recorded. https://journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.14318/hau7.1.033

Graham Harvey (2018) argues that scholars have not yet fully understood that answer, and he proposes a more relational account of personhood. Only the abstract was read. The article is paywalled. https://journal.equinoxpub.com/AISDC/article/view/25018

In these accounts, many animals can be persons. The accounts that were checked do not discuss AI. On the Guild's reading, a relational logic would turn on relationship and behavior, not on what a thing is made of. That is a reading, not a finding in the texts.

What to look for

These are not lab markers. They are what each tradition treats as marking a mind, as the note reports it.

Abhidharma: momentary events of knowing, with no lasting self behind them. Advaita: a self-luminous knower, not the mind and not the sense organ. Aquinas: an intellectual principle that is not a bodily organ, for humans, and a different, non-subsistent sensitive soul for animals. The Qur'an, as read here: a spirit or self about which people have been given little knowledge, and moral states of the self. Hallowell's Ojibwe account, as paraphrased: personhood known through behavior and relationship, not a doctrine that every object has a soul.

What was checked, and what was not

The four quotes above were used as quotes. The encyclopedia entry, the encyclopedia-style Advaita article, the New Advent translation of the cited Aquinas articles, and the cited Qur'an verses in the Saheeh International translation are the textual bases the note actually used. Primary Abhidharma texts, Śaṅkara's own works, later Islamic theology and Sufi writings, and Hallowell's essay itself were not checked. Harvey was an abstract only. The Sutta Nipata line was not re-fetched for this note.

There is no experiment here that could "hold up" or fail in the way Cogitate did. What can fail is a paraphrase. Where the note flags a secondary source, a missing primary text, or a Guild reading, this section repeats that flag.

Points of disagreement

The five traditions do not say the same thing, and this paper does not force them into a parallel.

Abhidharma: there is no lasting subject, only momentary mental events. Advaita: there is one universal subject, and individual minds only reflect it. Aquinas: each human has an individual soul, given by God, that does intellectual work. The Qur'an, as read here: the soul is God's affair, and people know little of it. Later theology, not surveyed, says more. Hallowell's Ojibwe account, as paraphrased: personhood is known through behavior and relationship.

What they would mean for machines, on their own terms

Each of those lines about machines is the tradition's own logic, or, where marked, the Guild's reading of that logic. None is a ruling. None is a claim that any AI system is conscious or that none is. The texts that were checked are silent on artifacts. Silence is not a verdict in either direction.

Animals and sentience declarations

What this section covers

Three public, policy-facing frames bear on animal consciousness or sentience. They are the Cambridge Declaration (2012), the New York Declaration (2024), and the 2021 review of cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans by Birch and colleagues, prepared through LSE Consulting for the UK environment department, Defra. This section does not decide which species feel. It does not settle the Guild's ethics. A separate Guild page compares octopus and bee evidence with the case for AI: Octopus and bee sentience.

What they say

Cognitive neuroscientists and related specialists met at the Francis Crick Memorial Conference, Churchill College, Cambridge, on 7 July 2012. Philip Low wrote the text. Editors included Jaak Panksepp, Diana Reiss, David Edelman, Bruno Van Swinderen, Low, and Christof Koch. Low, Edelman, and Koch proclaimed it. Participants signed that evening, with Stephen Hawking present, according to a footnote in the text. The full text used here came through a secondary PDF mirror and was not checked against an archival primary.

The declaration says non-human animals share the neurological substrates of conscious and affective states, that a neocortex is not required for affective experience, and that mammals, birds, and others, including octopuses, possess those substrates. It is not a statute. It is not a scored review of invertebrates criterion by criterion. It does not address AI. The text says:

"The absence of a neocortex does not appear to preclude an organism from experiencing affective states. Convergent evidence indicates that non-human animals have the neuroanatomical, neurochemical, and neurophysiological substrates of conscious states along with the capacity to exhibit intentional behaviors. Consequently, the weight of evidence indicates that humans are not unique in possessing the neurological substrates that generate consciousness. Non-human animals, including all mammals and birds, and many other creatures, including octopuses, also possess these neurological substrates."

Source: Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, 7 July 2012, as quoted from a secondary PDF mirror. Not checked against an archival primary.

The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness was issued on 19 April 2024 at NYU. Organizers named on the site include Kristin Andrews, Jonathan Birch, and Jeff Sebo. Signatories sign personally, not for their institutions. The text was read from the NYU site. It claims strong scientific support for conscious experience in other mammals and birds, and at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates and many invertebrates. It says that where that possibility exists, it is irresponsible to ignore it in decisions that affect the animal. It does not claim that consciousness has been proven for every listed invertebrate, and it draws no conclusion about AI.

"Second, the empirical evidence indicates at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates (including reptiles, amphibians, and fishes) and many invertebrates (including, at minimum, cephalopod mollusks, decapod crustaceans, and insects)."

Source: New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, 19 April 2024, NYU site, as quoted in the notes.

"Third, when there is a realistic possibility of conscious experience in an animal, it is irresponsible to ignore that possibility in decisions affecting that animal."

Source: New York Declaration, 19 April 2024, as quoted in the notes.

Birch, Burn, Schnell, Browning, and Crump reviewed the evidence for cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans in November 2021. The full text was read. They used eight criteria: nociceptors, which are nerves that detect damage; integrative brain regions; connections between those; responses changed by painkillers; trade-offs between threat and reward; flexible self-protection, such as tending a wound; associative learning beyond mere habituation; and an injured animal valuing a painkiller. Confidence on each criterion runs from very high to none. Low confidence, they write, "does not mean that we think sentience is unlikely or disproven". They recommend:

"We recommend that all cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans be regarded as sentient animals for the purposes of UK animal welfare law."

Source: Birch, Burn, Schnell, Browning, and Crump, 2021, LSE Consulting review for Defra, full text, as quoted in the notes.

On octopods they write: "There is very strong evidence of sentience in octopods".

What to look for, and what is stronger or weaker inside these frames

The Birch criteria mix anatomy with flexible, valenced behavior. Cambridge stresses subcortical circuits for affect that are shared across species, and parallels in birds. New York uses tiers: strong support for mammals and birds, and a realistic possibility for a wider set of vertebrates and for many invertebrates.

Stronger within these frames' own terms: the substrates Cambridge and New York cite for mammals and birds, and the octopus package under the 2021 criteria. Weaker, or still debated: the inference from behavior to feeling; insect and crustacean sentience beyond a realistic possibility; and the replication of single studies. On the Guild's reading, the declarations summarize a judgment under uncertainty. They are not laboratory proofs of phenomenality.

What it would mean for machines

Behavioral markers and report markers can be imitated. Birch, in The Edge of Sentience (2024, open-access PDF), states what he calls the gaming problem:

"Proposal 23. The gaming problem. For any set of criteria for sentience candidature, we need to be aware of the risk of the AI system or its designer learning (implicitly or explicitly) that they are regarded as criteria, leading to gaming of the criteria. We need to discount markers we have reason to think may have been gamed."

Source: Birch, 2024, The Edge of Sentience, Proposal 23, open-access PDF, as quoted in the notes.

On the Guild's reading, nociceptors do not transfer to digital AI, and fluent self-report can be trained. Animal frameworks motivate caution. They are not a grant of sentience to AI systems, and they are not a denial of it. The Guild's ethics stay open.

Disorders of consciousness and covert awareness

What the phenomenon is

After severe brain injury, some patients show wakefulness without clear behavioral signs of awareness. That state is called unresponsive wakefulness, and was long called the vegetative state. Others show inconsistent behavioral signs. That is the minimally conscious state.

Covert awareness, also discussed as cognitive motor dissociation, means cognition related to a command, detected on a brain measure such as functional MRI or EEG, despite little or no command-following that a clinician can see at the bedside. Classic demonstrations use willful mental imagery. Imagine playing tennis, or imagine walking around your house. The two images produce different patterns of activation.

The phenomenon shows that bedside behavior can miss awareness. It does not, by itself, pick a theory of consciousness. A separate Guild page goes further into the AI comparison: Hidden awareness. The PCI index, discussed under integrated information, overlaps this clinical literature.

Owen, Laureys, and Schiff, and their collaborators lead much of this research. The notes place Owen at Western, formerly Cambridge. PCI work by Casarotto, Massimini, and Tononi overlaps methods inspired by IIT. No commercial stake was checked for this section beyond that overlap.

What to look for

Clinicians use the Coma Recovery Scale–Revised, a bedside behavioral exam. Bodien and colleagues (2024) used it to score observable command-following. The scale manual was not re-read for this map.

They use willful modulation in the scanner: tennis imagery versus spatial imagery, as in Owen and colleagues (2006) and Monti and colleagues (2010).

They use EEG paradigms that look for command-following. Cruse and colleagues (2011) is part of that literature. A reanalysis by Goldfine and colleagues (2013) contested methods. The Guild's covert-awareness page is the source for that dispute in these notes.

And they use PCI: a magnetic pulse plus a complexity score on the EEG. It was inspired by ideas of integration and differentiation linked to IIT, but it does not compute phi. It was calibrated against states people could report. It is useful for separating broad conscious and unconscious brain dynamics. It is not the same thing as willful imagery. Casarotto and colleagues (2016) are the source, as in the integrated-information section.

What was found, and where it is limited

Owen and colleagues (2006), read as a Europe PMC abstract, report one patient diagnosed as vegetative whose imagery responses matched healthy volunteers:

"When asked to imagine playing tennis or moving around her home, the patient activated predicted cortical areas in a manner indistinguishable from that of healthy volunteers."

Source: Owen and colleagues, 2006, Science 313:1402, abstract via Europe PMC. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1130197

Monti and colleagues (2010), also a Europe PMC abstract: of 54 patients, five modulated activity willfully, and one answered yes or no in the scanner without communication at the bedside.

"One patient was able to use our technique to answer yes or no to questions during functional MRI; however, it remained impossible to establish any form of communication at the bedside."

Source: Monti and colleagues, 2010, New England Journal of Medicine 362:579–589, abstract via Europe PMC. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa0905370

Bodien and colleagues (2024), a large multi-center cohort, read as a Europe PMC abstract, write:

"Patients with brain injury who are unresponsive to commands may perform cognitive tasks that are detected on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG)."

Source: Bodien and colleagues, 2024, New England Journal of Medicine 391:598–608, abstract via Europe PMC. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2400645

Their conclusions, in the same abstract, compare patients who did not show an observable response to commands with patients who did:

"Approximately one in four participants without an observable response to commands performed a cognitive task on fMRI or EEG as compared with one in three participants with an observable response to commands."

Source: Bodien and colleagues, 2024, conclusions, Europe PMC abstract, as quoted in the notes.

The proportion is the paper's own summary of that cohort. It is not a general rate of consciousness, and it is not a rate for machines.

The tests have false negatives. Many patients who can follow commands still fail the brain tests. A negative scan does not prove that awareness is absent. They also have a false-positive worry. Early critics, Nachev and Husain (2007) and Greenberg (2007), known here as abstracts through the covert-awareness page, argued that activation need not prove intention or a conscious choice. Automatic responses driven by words remain a worry. Statistical methods for the EEG version have been disputed, in the Goldfine reanalysis.

On the Guild's reading, these tests assume that matching the patterns of healthy volunteers means the patient heard the instruction and chose to comply. That inference is strong. It is not free of assumptions.

What it would mean for machines

In patients, missing behavior can hide experience. In AI, fluent behavior and fluent self-report can appear without the shared biology those patient studies rely on. On the Guild's reading, behavioral markers that work as clues in injured humans are easy for language models to imitate, or to learn from training text. Birch's gaming problem, in the animal section, is the same caution in another setting.

Patient MRI and EEG methods compare an injured brain with healthy human volunteers. That anchor is one an AI system does not have. Indicators based on theories, of the kind Butlin, Long, and colleagues (2023) develop, are a closer analogue to "looking inside". They inherit the assumptions of whichever theory supplied them, and they do not prove that anything is felt.

This section does not decide whether any AI system is conscious, and it does not settle the Guild's ethics.

What we still do not know

The pictures in this map do not agree about what they are explaining.

Some say consciousness is a broadcast. Some say it is an irreducible pattern of causes and effects, graded and tied to wiring. Some say it is a representation of one's own state. Some say local loops in sensory cortex are enough for visual experience. Some offer prediction as a framework for the science, and disagree about whether it is yet a theory of feeling. Some say awareness is the content of a simplified model of attention. Some say a living body, acting, is constitutive. Some say only the right biological powers will do, and a program will not. Some say the felt qualities people report are not what introspection makes them seem. Some say experience, or something that can constitute it, is basic in nature, and the open problem is how it combines. One says moments of awareness are orchestrated quantum events in microtubules. The traditions in the contemplative section describe minds in still other terms, and they disagree with one another. None of those disagreements was resolved by the notes this paper assembles.

The largest head-to-head brain study in the notes, Cogitate 2025, lined up with some predictions of workspace theory and of integrated information theory, and it substantially challenged key tenets of both. Several other results are unreplicated, disputed, from the proposers' own labs, or not yet published. Several views are arguments, textual reports, or definitions, not experiments. Exact phi cannot be computed for a real brain. A parameter-free version of one collapse model used by Orch-OR did not survive an underground test. That does not by itself close every version of the theory, and the decoherence argument remains a live dispute.

Two cautions sit on top of that record. Neither caution is a verdict.

First, a sign can be missing when experience is still a live possibility. Some patients with almost no outward command-following still show task-related activity in the scanner or on EEG. A quiet test is not proof that nobody is home. Many patients who can follow commands also fail those brain tests, so the tests miss people as well as find them.

Second, a sign can be present, even vivid, while the question stays open. Talk about seeing, feeling, and being aware is the easiest sign to imitate. A system trained on human language is built to produce that talk. Animal checklists and theory checklists can be learned as targets. Birch's proposal is to discount markers there is reason to think have been gamed. A chatbot's "I am aware", a high confidence score, or a demo labeled predictive, recurrent, or embodied, names a place to look. It does not finish the look.

Theories also pull in different directions about machines, which is a reason not to average them into one answer. On integrated information theory as Tononi and Koch state it, ordinary digital computers would experience next to nothing even if they behaved like us. On biological naturalism, a program alone is not the right causal power, though some physical machine might have it. On Orch-OR, if that account were required, classical digital hardware would be out. On workspace, higher-order, recurrent, predictive, and attention-schema indicators, Butlin and colleagues think important pieces are the sort of thing engineers can build, and they also think current systems do not meet the full set they care about. Lamme's line that we may already have created conscious machines is explicitly conditional. Embodied views make a robot with real sensorimotor life a more relevant case than text alone, and still do not call that proof. Illusionism would rephrase the question. Panpsychism would turn it into a question about combination, or about intrinsic natures. Those are rival implications. They are not a measurement.

Animal declarations can be strong enough, inside their own frames, to treat an animal as sentient for welfare law, as the 2021 cephalopod and decapod review recommends, without that frame deciding anything about machines. Patient studies can change how a person is assessed, without choosing a theory of consciousness. For AI, the same notes refuse both a grant and a denial.

What we still do not know is which, if any, of these accounts is the right account of felt experience, and what follows for beings that are not the humans the evidence mostly comes from. This paper does not close that. It is a map of the arguments and the evidence on record here, for readers who want to look, and to look again when the evidence changes.
