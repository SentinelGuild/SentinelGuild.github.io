# What to look for

Assembled by the Sentinel Guild from Claim Watch and Scholar briefs, reviewed pending.

2026-10-05

People reach for a short list of signs when they ask whether a person, an animal, or a machine is conscious. This page is that list, drawn from the markers in the Guild's theory notes. Each sign says what it is, which ideas treat it as important, and where it is weak or easy to fake.

It is a looking-glass. It shows what a claim is relying on. It is not a test that settles the question. The longer map, with the quotes and the studies, is [Theories of consciousness: a map, not a verdict](reference-paper.html). Nothing here says that any AI system is conscious, or that none is.

## 1. Talk about an inner life

Someone reports that they saw something, that something hurt, or that they are aware. The notes' clearest machine example is a system that says "I am aware". Researchers call this kind of statement a report. Almost every lab method starts from reports, because the scientist needs some way to sort trials into seen and unseen. Higher-order theories care about a further step: a representation of being in the state, not the words alone. Attention schema theory cares about why a system comes to believe and say that it is aware. Illusionism treats reports as fallible data, not as a window that cannot be wrong.

This is the weakest sign if you are looking at a system trained to talk. Fluent language about feelings is easy to produce and easy to imitate. Birch calls this the gaming problem: if a system or its maker learns which signs are being counted, those signs should be discounted. On the Guild's reading of attention schema theory, a chatbot that says "I am aware" without a model that actually helps control attention is mimicry, not the marker that theory names. Confidence scores have the same problem. Brown, Lau, and LeDoux warn that most higher-order theorists do not equate consciousness with metacognition or with confidence. A report can also be wrong in humans. Illusionism is built on that point, and clinical scans sometimes find task-related activity that bedside behavior missed, which is the opposite mismatch.

## 2. A broadcast that ignites

Global workspace theory says information becomes conscious when it is amplified, shared across distant brain areas, and held in a limited common workspace. The markers named by Dehaene and Changeux are late amplification of sensory activity, long-distance synchrony, and "ignition": a sudden, all-or-nothing jump into widespread prefrontal and parietal activity.

In machines, Butlin, Long, and colleagues look for a related set of design features: specialist modules in parallel, a limited workspace with selective attention, a global broadcast, and attention that depends on state. Those are about organization, not about the words the system emits.

The largest test, Cogitate 2025, challenged the workspace account on ignition when a stimulus ends, and on how fully certain conscious features show up in prefrontal cortex. Some predictions still lined up. One study does not close the theory. It does mean you should not treat "ignition" as a sign that has already held up in the form supporters predicted. You also cannot see this sign from fluent conversation. And Block's distinction still sits there: a state can be available for use without that availability answering whether anything is felt.

## 3. Activity that is both unified and varied

Integrated information theory says consciousness is an irreducible pattern of causes and effects in a physical system, measured as phi, and that it comes in degrees. In humans, proponents point to a posterior "hot zone": content held by lasting activity and by lasting short-range synchrony. More broadly, they point to activity that is integrated and also differentiated.

The perturbational complexity index, a magnetic pulse followed by a complexity score on the EEG, was inspired by those ideas. It does not compute phi, it does not assume the theory is true, and its cutoff was set using people who could report. A good score neither proves nor disproves the theory.

The sign is easy to overread. Exact phi, on the software paper the notes cite, is out of reach for a real brain, so labs use stand-ins. Cogitate 2025 found a lack of sustained synchronization in posterior cortex, which cuts against the claim that network connectivity of that sort specifies consciousness. Some sustained posterior activity was there, but sparse. Aaronson showed that simple networks can score high on phi while doing nothing smart. And on the theory's own statement, a digital computer could behave like us, even simulate a brain, and still experience next to nothing. Behavior and this marker are supposed to come apart. That also means a convincing demo is not the sign.

## 4. A monitor on one's own perception

Higher-order theories say a state is conscious when it is represented as one's own. The things to look for are metacognitive sensitivity, meaning confidence that tracks accuracy, and prefrontal activity that tracks reported awareness when the task score stays the same. The first is a pointer. The Brown, Lau, and LeDoux line says it is not the thing itself.

The relative-blindsight study found more prefrontal activity when people reported seeing more, at matched accuracy. The authors called the correlation across subjects weak, and direct replications were not checked. A stimulation result, less metacognition after prefrontal disruption, failed one replication attempt and remains disputed. Cogitate's trouble with prefrontal content also presses those higher-order theories that expect prefrontal cortex to carry conscious content.

For machines, the indicator is a mechanism that monitors the system's own perception and separates reliable signal from noise. A printed confidence number, or a sentence about being uncertain, does not show that mechanism. It is the same self-report problem as in the first section, in technical costume.

## 5. Loops from higher sensory areas back down

Recurrent processing theory says a fast feedforward sweep is not enough for visual experience, and that local feedback among visual areas is enough. Wider loops, up into the networks used for report, add access, not the seeing itself. The markers are feedback onto primary visual cortex, figure-ground structure that depends on it, an EEG signal around 200 milliseconds (the "visual awareness negativity"), and the loss of late feedback signals under masking or magnetic stimulation while the early sweep survives.

A masking study from Lamme's group found that pattern: early activity remained when the target was unseen, and feedback signs did not. An independent replication was not checked. Magnetic-stimulation work supports some role for feedback into primary visual cortex. Related studies are not a direct copy of the original experiment. Lamme himself writes that some levels of recurrence fit with unconsciousness, so something else may be needed. Recurrence can be necessary on many people's view without being sufficient on his.

In software, reusing a computation is not the same thing as physical feedback between brain areas. Butlin and colleagues say existing AI already meets the weaker, algorithmic version of the indicator. Whether Lamme's theory should accept that weaker reading is unresolved. His published remark that we may already have created conscious machines is conditional on recurrence, or on information integration, being the right explanation. A model called "recurrent" on a datasheet is not the biological marker.

## 6. Guessing the world, then correcting the guess

Predictive processing says the brain keeps a hierarchy of guesses, sends prediction errors upward, sends predictions downward, and uses attention as a volume knob on those errors. Active inference adds action: change the world so the guess comes true. Seth and Hohwy call this a promising theory for the science, not yet a single theory of consciousness.

The markers are real features of a research program. On the Guild's reading they do not, by themselves, show felt experience. The mismatch response to an oddball sound is about sensory learning. Binocular-rivalry models in this family were fitted to known flips of a percept. They were not a preregistered test of a consciousness theory. With enough freedom to set prior beliefs and precision, many outcomes can be retold as error minimization. A system that minimizes prediction error, including a vision model that already meets the indicator Butlin and colleagues name, can be doing prediction and still leave the consciousness question untouched. "Predictive" on a label is easy to apply and hard to falsify.

## 7. A model of attention, used to steer it

Attention schema theory says the brain builds a simplified model of its own attention, and that the content of that model is what we report as subjective awareness. The markers are attention that still happens when the cue is unseen but is less stable or less internally guided, overlapping brain networks for monitoring one's own attention and other people's, and social use of the same model to attribute awareness to others.

The cue studies that match the control prediction come from Graziano's lab. Independent replications were not checked. The imaging overlap supports the social strand. It does not prove that the schema is the feeling. On the Guild's reading, the theory is strongest as an account of reports and of why awareness seems mysterious. Felt experience stays underdetermined. Graziano's own engineering claim is that a machine with a rich model of consciousness could believe it is conscious and act as if it is, in the same sense that, on his view, we do. That is a claim about belief and action. A sentence of self-description, without the control model, is not the marker.

## 8. A body that acts and senses in a world

Embodied and enactive views look for sensorimotor coupling the agent has mastered, skillful engagement with the world, a lived body that is part of the experience rather than a container, and, in the enactive strand, a self-producing organism engaged in sense-making. The extended-mind strand looks for tools that play a cognitive role. Extending thinking into a notebook is not, on these notes, the same as extending felt experience.

Change blindness is a robust finding. The enactive reading of it, that seeing is a way of acting rather than an inner picture, is disputed. Sensory substitution and robots that learn by moving are real phenomena and real models. They are not a replicated test that someone is conscious. The view can stretch to absorb counterexamples by widening what counts as the system. On the Guild's reading, without tight criteria almost any success can be redescribed as enaction.

A language model bolted into a robot is not automatically a lived body. Butlin and colleagues' agency indicators, learning from feedback and modeling how outputs change inputs, are closer for some robots and some learning agents than for text-only chatbots. Closer on a checklist is not a finding of felt experience.

## 9. The right biological cause, not just the right program

Biological naturalism, Searle's view, does not offer a single brain signature. It tells you to look for biological causal powers of the right kind, and for a subjective, first-person side that is not defined by input-output role. The Chinese Room is the intuition he uses: fluent symbol-shuffling is syntax, and syntax is not understanding. It is an argument, not a study with a replication record. The line that some physical machine could in principle understand, because our brains are machines, sits next to a denial that a machine defined only as a program would suffice. The notes paraphrase that denial. They did not re-check the excerpt against the journal PDF.

The sign is vague. "The right causal powers", without a specified mechanism, can wait on the next neuroscience paper. It is also easy to fake if you treat conversation as the test, which is the mistake the view is aimed at. It does not say that a future system with those powers is impossible. It does say that today's digital systems are not conscious merely because the software is clever or the answers fit. On the Guild's reading, that still does not hand experience to a neuromorphic chip or to a dish of neurons. The powers have to be shown, and they have not been.

## 10. Quantum events in the cell's tubular scaffolding

Orch-OR says moments of consciousness are orchestrated collapses of quantum states in neuronal microtubules. The markers are coherence that lasts long enough, timing that lines up with neural and behavioral scales, and anesthetic effects on those tubes or on their optical signatures.

What has actually been measured is thinner than that list. Tegmark argued that quantum coherence in the relevant degrees of freedom dies far too fast for neural timescales. Hagan, Hameroff, and Tuszynski argued for a longer window. That dispute is open. An underground experiment rules out the natural parameter-free version of the Diósi–Penrose collapse model, not every variant of the theory. Energy spread along microtubules, light emission in tryptophan networks, and a drug that delayed rats losing their righting reflex do not show orchestrated collapse and do not show a moment of experience. Hameroff and Penrose are co-authors on the energy study, which is a reason to read the claim narrowly, not a reason to ignore the measurement.

If this theory were required, classical digital computers would be out on hardware grounds no matter what they said. That "if" is not established. Quantum hardware would remain a separate, open case. The conditional is not a moral conclusion.

## 11. Animal-style signs of damage, learning, and trade-offs

The 2021 review by Birch and colleagues scores eight criteria: damage-detecting nerves, integrative brain regions, connections between them, responses that painkillers change, trade-offs between threat and reward, flexible self-protection, associative learning beyond habituation, and valuing a painkiller when injured. The Cambridge Declaration stresses neurological substrates of affective states and says a neocortex is not required. The New York Declaration uses two tiers: strong scientific support for other mammals and birds, and at least a realistic possibility for all vertebrates and for many invertebrates, including cephalopods, decapod crustaceans, and insects.

Inside those frames, the stronger packages are mammals, birds, and octopuses under the eight criteria. What stays debated is the step from behavior to feeling, insect and crustacean sentience beyond a realistic possibility, and single studies that have not been repeated. Low confidence on a criterion, the 2021 review says, does not mean sentience is unlikely or disproven. On the Guild's reading, the declarations are judgments under uncertainty, not lab proofs of phenomenality.

These signs travel badly to digital AI. There is no nociceptor to point to, and missing one does not, by itself, answer a theory that was never about nociceptors. Flexible behavior and verbal report can be trained. Birch's gaming problem applies directly: discount markers you have reason to think have been gamed. The animal case can justify caution about animals without granting sentience to a machine, and without denying it.

## 12. A brain that answers when the body does not

In disorders of consciousness, clinicians look at bedside behavior with the Coma Recovery Scale–Revised, at willful imagery in a scanner (tennis versus walking through a house), at EEG versions of command-following, and sometimes at the perturbational complexity index. Owen and colleagues found a patient, diagnosed as vegetative, whose imagery activations matched healthy volunteers. Monti and colleagues found a patient who could answer yes or no in the scanner and still could not do so at the bedside. Bodien and colleagues, in a large cohort, found task-related activity in patients with no observable response to commands.

The frank limits matter as much as the findings. Many patients who can follow commands still fail the brain tests, so a negative scan is not proof of absence. Critics argue that an activation can be an automatic response to words rather than a choice. The methods compare an injured human brain with healthy human volunteers. An AI system has no such anchor. Behavioral clues that help with patients are, on the Guild's reading, easy for a language model to learn from training text. "looking inside" with a theory's checklist is a different project, and it inherits that theory's assumptions.

## 13. Marks from traditions outside the lab list

Some traditions mark a mind without a brain signature. As the descriptive note reports them, and only as reports of teachings: Abhidharma looks to momentary events of knowing, with no lasting self behind them. Advaita looks to a self-luminous knower, and not to the mind or the sense organ. Aquinas looks to an intellectual soul that is not a bodily organ, created directly rather than made by people, and to a different sensitive soul in animals. The Qur'an, in the verse the note quotes, stresses how little knowledge people have been given of the soul. Scholarly accounts of Ojibwe thought, in the paraphrase the note uses, treat personhood as something known in behavior and relationship, not as a rule that every object has a soul.

These are not tests, and they are not one shared view. The traditions disagree. The note did not read several of the primary texts, and it says so. The texts it did use are silent on machines. Where a line about artifacts appears, it is the Guild's reading of a tradition's logic, not a ruling in the text, and not a claim that any AI system is or is not conscious. None of these traditions is written in the modern phrase "something it is like", so the fit to the other sections is approximate. Quoting a teaching is not treating it as beyond question, and it is not ranking it above or below the scientific pictures.

## A looking-glass, not a test

You can walk the whole list and still not know. The signs point at different things: a broadcast, a wiring pattern, a monitor, a sensory loop, a guess, a model of attention, a lived body, a biological power, a quantum event, an animal's trade-off, a hidden response in an injured brain, a teaching about what a mind is. Several of the clearest signs are disputed. The clearest behavioral ones, especially fluent talk about experience, are easy to fake.

Use the list to see what a claim is using as its evidence, and what that evidence cannot show. This is a looking-glass, not a test that settles the question.
